Purpose Alzheimer's disease (AD) induces changes in retinal structure/function, making the retina a suitable platform to study the molecular mechanisms of the disease. Dysregulation of some microRNAs (miRNAs) has also been found in AD pathogenesis. Here, we used the 5xFAD mouse to expand our knowledge on structural, functional, and molecular retinal alterations and to elucidate the retinal miRNA profile in this model of AD. Methods The 5xFAD mice at 3, 6, or 9 months of age, were used. Retinal function was evaluated with electroretinogram (ERG) and the Prusky water maze test. Retinal structure was investigated by optical coherence tomography. Molecular analyses included immunohistochemistry, Western blot, and ELISA. Retinal miRNAs were profiled and deregulated miRNAs were validated by qRT-PCR. Results Starting from 6 months, the 5xFAD mice showed altered ERG and visual acuity. The inner plexiform layer became thicker whereas the retinal ganglion cell (RGC) layer became thinner. In the RGC layer, the accumulation of amyloid beta was concomitant with RGC apoptosis, whereas tau protein phosphorylation was increased. Inflammatory processes were also activated and microgliosis became apparent. Five deregulated miRNAs were identified, four of which were validated. Two of these miRNAs were related to AD and involved in gene expression relevant to retinal function. Conclusions The present findings confirm and expand our knowledge of the retinal disease in 5xFAD mice, and highlight that neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, and microgliosis participate in AD pathogenesis. It is characterized by abnormal processing and clearance of amyloid beta (Aβ) and hyperphosphorylation of tau protein, leading to the formation of Aβ plaques and neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs). 1 These neurotoxic species contribute to the development of many pathological processes, including microgliosis, neuroinflammation, and oxidative stress, eventually resulting in impaired synaptic transmission and neuronal death. 2 – 4 A definitive AD diagnosis can only come from a histopathological examination of postmortem brain sections, although accumulation of cerebral Aβ deposition is underway decades before the appearance of clinical symptoms. 5 Positron emission tomography and cerebrospinal fluid assays can detect AD biomarkers many years before the clinical onset of the pathology, but these tests are either invasive or expensive. 6 – 8 Patients with AD are commonly reported to suffer from visual impairments that are, at least in part, ascribable to AD-related retinal damage. 9 – 14 The neural retina is part of the central nervous system (CNS), and it shares common embryological origins and similar morpho-functional features with cortical neurons. The retina is easily accessible for noninvasive, high-resolution imaging 15 , 16 and for recording the functional activity of its neurons through various electroretinographic techniques. 17 – 19 These properties make the retina a suitable platform to study the pathophysiological mechanisms of CNS disorders, such as AD. Although some studies failed to identify Aβ plaques in the retinas of patients with AD, 20 – 22 many others reported the presence of such inclusions, highlighting a correlation between Aβ deposits and degeneration of retinal ganglion cells (RGCs). 23 – 27 Aβ retinal plaques mostly appear in the inner retinal layers, including the ganglion cell layer (GCL), inner plexiform layer (IPL), and inner nuclear layer (INL), 24 even though Aβ accumulation was also detected in the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE), where it can trigger an increase in oxidative stress and inflammation, indirectly affecting photoreceptor activity. 28 Optic nerve degenerations are often observed, 29 – 31 whereas GCL and retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) thickness is reduced, 24 , 32 – 36 even though thickening of the IPL may also occur. 37 These pathological changes result in retinal dysfunction, as evidenced by electroretinogram (ERG) analyses. 38 An important addition to the knowledge of retinal conditions in AD would be the elucidation of the retinal microRNA (miRNA) profiles. Indeed, miRNA-based therapeutic strategies may have an important impact in the management of multifactorial diseases, such as AD, considering that, in these pathologies, the classical pharmacological approach “one target-one drug” may not be enough, because several molecular pathways are dysregulated, and miRNAs can regulate multiple targets of various molecular cascades. 39 , 40 Many central players in AD pathogenesis, such as amyloid precursor protein (APP), 41 – 46 β-secretase, 41 , 47 – 50 apolipoprotein E, 51 and tau protein, 47 , 52 – 56 are known to be directly or indirectly regulated by miRNA. The miRNA profiles in the brain, cerebrospinal fluid, and plasma of patients with AD have been extensively characterized, highlighting their altered expression compared with healthy individuals. 57 – 61 Nevertheless, identifying putative retinal miRNome alterations could provide new insights into the key pathways regulated by miRNAs in AD pathogenesis, leading to the identification of potential novel therapeutic targets, which could be more easily addressed in their own testbed (i.e. the retina) compared to the brain. Thus, in the present work, we performed a series of cross-sectional studies on the transgenic mouse with five familial AD mutations in the 5xFAD murine model to (i) recapitulate and expand the existent knowledge of the AD-related progressive functional, morphological, and molecular alterations concerning the retina, and (ii) propose a new approach of AD investigation, such as the elucidation of retinal miRNA profile. Materials and Methods Animals The 5xFAD murine model expresses five familial AD mutations, of which three are on the human APP gene (Florida I716V, Swedish K670N/M671L, and London V717I) and two are on the human presenilin 1 (PSEN1) gene (L286V and M146L), each driven by the mouse thymus cell antigen 1 (Thy1) promoter, a neuron-selective promoter widely used for in vivo neuronal transduction to generate transgenic animals with neuron-specific expression of target genes. 62 – 63 Due to these mutations, 5xFAD mice quickly develop severe and progressive amyloid pathology, with plaques appearing in the brain starting at 2 months of age, triggering microgliosis and inflammation as well as synaptic and neuronal loss. 63 – 68 Deficits in spatial learning start at 4 months of age, generally followed by synaptic impairment in the hippocampus at approximately 6 months of age. 63 , 66 , 68 Male 5xFAD mice (B6.Cg-Tg(APPSwFlLon,PSEN1*M146L*L286V)6799Vas/Mmjax) were purchased from Jackson Labs (Bar Harbor, ME, USA; stock # 008730). In origin, both transgenes were co-injected in cells of C57BL/6XSJL hybrid embryos, and founders were bred with B6SJL mice. Because SJL mice express the retinal degeneration allele phosphodiesterase-6b retinal degeneration-1 ( Pde6b rd1 ) that does not allow the use of transgenic mice for retinal studies, transgenic mice were then backcrossed to C57BL/6J mice to breed out of the strain the retinal degeneration allele. Male 5xFAD mice were bred in our animal facilities with female C57BL/6J mice (Jackson Labs). The mice were genotyped for APP and PSEN1 transgenes. In addition, the absence of the Pde6b rd1 allele was verified using polymerase chain reaction (PCR) with specific primers (Jackson Labs). Non-carrier mice obtained from mating male 5xFAD mice with female C57BL/6J mice were used as wild-type (WT) littermate controls. In the present study, we used 3-, 6-, and 9-month-old 5xFAD mice. A total number of 102 mice (51 WT and 51 5xFAD) was used. The present study was performed in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of the National Institutes of Health, the ARVO Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research, the Italian guidelines for animal care (DL 6/14, permission no. 864/2024-PR), and the European Communities Council Directive (2010/63/UE), and the principles of the 3Rs. Animals were housed under standard conditions of 12-hour cycles of light and dark, 23 ± 1°C, with food and water ad libitum. Electroretinography The mice were subjected to different types of electroretinography, including an analysis of the rod pathway with scotopic-ERG (scERG), an analysis of the cone pathway with photopic-ERG (phERG), and an analysis of RGC activity with pattern-ERG (PERG). After overnight dark adaptation, the mice were anesthetized with an intraperitoneal injection of avertin (1.2% avertin, 0.02 mL/g body weight; Sigma‐Aldrich) and placed in a custom-made stereotaxic apparatus allowing for an unobstructed visual field. The body temperature was maintained at 37°C with a feedback-controlled heating pad, while corneal moisture was maintained with a saline solution. Recording silver-silver chloride electrodes (0.2 mm loop-shaped) were laid on the corneal surface of each eye using micromanipulators, while stainless-steel needles were inserted subcutaneously in the frontal region and used as reference electrodes (one for each eye). Another stainless-steel needle was subcutaneously inserted at the base of the tail and used as the ground electrode. Flash stimuli were delivered with a Ganzfeld stimulator (CSO, Firenze, Italy), and the ERG recordings were acquired through a commercially available data acquisition device (Retimax Advanced; CSO). Scotopic responses were elicited using 10 cd-s/m 2 stimuli. Five consecutive signals recorded simultaneously from each eye were averaged to reduce noise after amplification (5000-fold) and subsequently band-pass filtered (0.1–100 hertz [Hz]). In the deriving waveform, the amplitude of the a-wave (baseline to trough) and that of the b-wave (trough to peak) were measured. Oscillatory potentials (OPs) were isolated from each endpoint scERG recording by band-pass filtering of 65 to 300 Hz to exclude interference from the a- and b-wave. The amplitudes of the main OPs (OP1, OP2, OP3, and OP4) were measured from the corresponding peaks to the preceding troughs, according to the International Society for Clinical Electrophysiology of Vision (ISCEV) guidelines. 69 Immediately after the acquisition of the scotopic responses, the animals were light-adapted for 60 seconds before recording photopic cone-mediated responses using 10 cd-s/m 2 stimuli on a 30 cd-s/m 2 rod-saturating green background light. The phERG responses simultaneously recorded from both eyes were amplified (10,000-fold) and band-pass filtered (1–30 Hz). The waveform deriving from the average of 50 consecutive responses was analyzed for the b-wave (baseline to peak) and the photopic negative response (phNR; baseline to first negative deflection after the b-wave). Following the phERG session, PERG responses were recorded. They were evoked by a visual stimulus consisting of 0.05 cycles/degree black and white (98% contrast) bars contrast-reversing at 1 Hz temporal frequency. The pattern stimuli were generated by a 19-inch light-emitting diode display (area = 74 degrees × 62 degrees) with a mean luminance of 50 cd/m 2 aligned at approximately 30 cm from the corneal surface. Each pattern reversal-deriving signal was amplified (10,000-fold) and band-pass filtered (1–30 Hz). A total of 300 consecutive pattern reversals were averaged to reduce noise contamination by a factor of 300 = 17.32. The PERG response was evaluated by measuring the amplitude of the N1 to P1 and P1 to N2 waves (from the trough of the negative peak = N1, to the peak of the positive peak = P1, and from the peak of the positive peak = P1, and to the trough of the negative peak = N2, respectively). Prusky Water Maze Test The Prusky water maze test is a behavioral test used for the quantitative measurement of visual discrimination in mice. It has been validated as an assay of visual acuity based on the animal's motivation driven by the natural instinct to escape the water, in which, unlike spatial navigation tasks such as the Morris Water Maze, visual function is assessed minimizing confounding factors like spatial learning and memory. 70 This test relies on the animal’s ability to discriminate high-contrast visual stimuli in real time. It is based on a binary forced-choice task in which the animal must swim to a monitor presenting a vertical grating pattern (target stimulus, associated with the presence of a hidden rescue platform), while avoiding another monitor presenting a uniform gray screen (neutral stimulus). The mice were initially trained at 2 months of age following a published protocol with stimuli at low spatial frequency (0.12 cycles per degree). The training was performed just before each test phase and it was repeated until the animals achieved near-perfect performance over 20 to 40 trials. 70 , 71 In the test phase, the visual stimuli had a constant luminance of 39.95 cd/m², with spatial frequencies ranging from 0.087 to 0.645 cycles/degree. The value of visual acuity was assessed as the highest spatial frequency at which at least 75% of the correct responses were recorded. Optical Coherence Tomography The in vivo assessment of retinal morphology was performed by image-guided optical coherence tomography (OCT) in WT and 5xFAD mice at 3, 6, and 9 months of age using the Micron IV system (Phoenix Research Laboratories, Pleasanton, CA, USA). The mice were anesthetized with an intraperitoneal injection of avertin (1.2% avertin, 0.02 mL/g body weight; Sigma‐Aldrich). Mydriasis was induced by 1% tropicamide, and 2% hydroxypropyl-methylcellulose drops were used to avoid eye drying. Bidimensional OCT images were acquired by image-guided circular scans (550 µm diameter) around the optic nerve head. The segmentation of retinal layers and the quantification of layer thickness were performed using Insight software (Phoenix Research Laboratories). Data from both eyes were averaged. Immunofluorescence Mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation. Both eyes were immediately enucleated and immersion-fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) 1 × for 2 hours at room temperature. The fixed eyes were transferred to 0.1 M PBS 1 × containing 25% sucrose and stored at 4°C until usage. Subsequently, the eyes were embedded in cryo-gel, frozen using liquid nitrogen, cut into 10-µm thick coronal sections with a cryostat, and mounted onto gelatin-covered glass slides. After rinsing, the slides were coverslipped with Fluoroshield mounting medium containing 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; Abcam). Images were viewed with an epifluorescence microscope (Ni-E; Nikon-Europe, Amsterdam, The Netherlands) and acquired with a DS-Fi1c digital camera (Nikon-Europe). To evaluate the RGC number, three retinal sections adjacent to the optic nerve head (central retina) were chosen from each retina (6 retinas per group). Retinal miRNome and Bioinformatic Analysis Following the manufacturer’s instructions, an enriched fraction of miRNAs was extracted from dissected retinas using the miRNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). Briefly, the retinas (5 samples for each time point for each strain, each containing 2 retinas from the same mouse) were homogenized in QIAzol Lysis Reagent using the Tissue Lyser instrument (Qiagen) and spun. The supernatants were added with chloroform and centrifuged again. The transparent supernatants were recovered and added with pure ethanol, transferred in the miRNeasy spin columns, and washed with the supplied buffers. The fraction of enriched miRNAs was then eluted in 30 µL RNase-free water. The quantity and purity of miRNAs were evaluated using Biophotometer D30 (Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany). The integrity of the samples was determined by RNA integrity number (RIN) evaluation using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Mountain View, CA, USA). Only samples with an RIN &gt;7 were selected for library preparation and sequencing that were performed by an external facility (Genartis, Castel d'Azzano, Italy). Libraries were prepared using the QIASeq miRNA library kit with an input of 100 ng/sample, a cycle of library amplification consisting of 16 PCR cycles. Libraries were sequenced single-end with an Illumina Sequencing NextSeq500 with a depth of 13M fragments/samples on average. The fragment size was 75 bp. After adapter sequences’ removal with cutadapt, 72 reads were aligned to miRBase, 73 with the SHRiMP aligner. 74 Supplementary Table S1 summarizes the sample sequencing statistics. After read count with UMI and count normalization, an analysis of differentially expressed miRNAs was performed using DEseq2, 75 applying the Apeglm shrinkage for LFC. Differentially expressed miRNAs were filtered using a cutoff of P &lt; 0.05 (statistically significant results). The analysis of miRNA target genes was performed using MultiMiR (1.26.0). 76 Entrez IDs of target genes were used to perform gene ontology through an over-representation analysis (ORA) by using the enrichGO function of the ClusterProfiler R package (version 4.10.1). 77 The ORA results were graphically represented using the cnetplot function of the ClusterProfiler R package. Validation of miRNA Expression On the same retinal samples used for miRNA profiling, miRNA expression was technically validated by quantitative RT-PCR (qRT-PCR) using a commercially available master mix (SsoAdvanced Universal SYBR Green Supermix; Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc.) with a reverse universal primer (Mir-X miRNA First-Strand Synthesis Kit, Takara, CA, USA) and miRNA-specific forward primers on a detection system (CFX Connect Real-Time PCR Detection System provided with the software CFX manager version 3.1; Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc.). The miRNA specific forward primer sequences were: miR-124-5p, 5ʹ-CGTGTTCACAGCGGACCTTGAT-3ʹ; miR-3968, 5ʹ-CGAATCCCACTCCAGACACCA-3ʹ; miR-3963, 5ʹ-TGTATCCCACTTCTGACAC-3ʹ; miR-3473f, 5ʹ-CAAATAGGACTGGAGAGATG-3ʹ; and miR-12191-3p, 5ʹ-CCCATGGAGCTGTAGGAGCCG-3ʹ. Results Retinal Function and Structure of 5xFAD Mice The electroretinographic routine displayed in Figure 1 was designed to obtain a comprehensive evaluation of the retinal function over time, including dark- and light-adapted photoreceptoral, post-photoreceptoral, and RGC activity in 5xFAD mice as compared with their WT counterparts. As depicted in Figure 1 A, 5xFAD mice show retinal activity comparable with WT mice at 3 months of age, whereas relevant changes were observed at later ages. The analysis of scERG highlighted that the amplitude of the a-wave (see Fig. 1 B), referring to the overall photoreceptoral activity, was significantly reduced in the 5xFAD mice at 9 months of age. In contrast, the b-wave (see Fig. 1 C), reflecting the overall post-photoreceptoral response, including bipolar cell and Müller cell activity, displayed significantly lower amplitudes in 5xFAD mice retinas both at 6 and 9 months of age. Overall, the scERG data indicated that in 5xFAD mice, inner retinal neurons were affected before evident functional deficits appeared in retinal photoreceptors. The phERG b-wave amplitudes, which represent the post-photoreceptoral activity mainly related to the cone pathway, did not show any significant variations between 5xFAD and WT mice either at 3 or at 6 months of age, but they were significantly reduced in 5xFAD animals at 9 months (see Fig. 1 D). Regarding the phNR amplitude, generally related to RGC activity, 78 it was significantly reduced in 5xFAD mice, compared to WT mice, at both 6 and 9 months of age (see Fig. 1 E). Overall, the phERG data were consistent with those of the scERG analysis and indicated that neurons of the inner retina displayed pathological functional changes before cone photoreceptors. PERG responses were recorded to investigate the functional activity of RGCs. Similar to the phNR data, both the N1 to P1 (see Fig. 1 F) and the P1 to N2 (see Fig. 1 G) amplitudes were significantly decreased in 5xFAD mice compared with WT mice, at 6 and 9 months of age. Overall, the PERG data indicated an evident and progressive decrease of RGC function in 5xFAD mice starting at or slightly before 6 months of age. Figure 1. Characterization of 5xFAD Mouse Retinas The ERG data indicated that, in 5xFAD mice, RGCs displayed pathological functional changes beginning at 6 months of age. Consistently, Aβ deposits were observed in the GCL of 5xFAD retinas at 6 months, and they were further increased at 9 months, when infiltration of the deposits toward internal retinal layers was also observed ( Figs. 5 A–D). Double label immunofluorescence indicated that, in 5xFAD retinas at 6 months, Aβ deposits were localized within RGCs and they were not in displaced amacrine cells ( Supplementary Fig. S2 ). As shown in Figure 5 I, measurements of the retinal Aβ content with ELISA confirmed significant increases of Aβ (1–42) in 5xFAD retinas at 6 and 9 months of age. To ascertain whether the increased presence of Aβ deposits in the GCL might be associated with changes in RGC viability, the number of RBPMS-immunolabeled RGCs was evaluated over time in WT and 5xFAD mice retinal sections taken from central retina ( Figs. 5 E–H). In line with data of Aβ immunofluorescence, a significant decrease in RGC number, compared to WT mice, was observed in 5xFAD retinas at 6 and 9 months of age ( Fig. 5 J). Figure 5. Discussion The present study provides an in-depth overview of the retinal morpho-functional and molecular features of the 5xFAD murine model of AD, describes the retinal miRNome of these mice, and identifies relevant dysregulated miRNAs potentially involved in AD-related retinal damage. The electroretinographic results show the presence of evident retinal functional alterations in 5xFAD mice involving both inner and outer retinal components, in line with the results of several research groups. 84 – 88 Generally, photoreceptor anomalies remain unclear in AD, but a progressive degeneration of rods and cones was reported in some AD murine models, in which impaired expression of phototransduction proteins, photoreceptor morphological alterations, functional deficits, and cell death caused by apoptosis, necroptosis, or senescence were reported. 89 , 90 Here, 5xFAD mice displayed a progressive alteration of scERG and phERG over time, indicating an overall photoreceptoral and post-receptoral dysfunction by 9 months of age. Photoreceptor dysfunction appeared belated during progression of the disease, although the ERG routine could not detect possible different susceptibility of rods and cones. Moreover, it was not possible to ascertain whether such alterations could be due to dysfunction in photon capture or to cell loss because the single irradiance experimental design did not allow such analysis. Conversely, parameters related to post-receptoral responses (scERG b-wave, phNR, PERG, and OPs) were altered early in 5xFAD mice retinas. These data point to an earlier functional failure of the post-receptoral retina, followed by a later dysfunction of photoreceptor responses. Given that PERG and phNR are mainly related to RGC activity, 91 and we show a significant reduction in RGC number in FAD mice, these results suggest that overall output of the RGCs to the brain is reduced both by dysfunction of inputs from bipolar, Müller, and amacrines cells, as well as degeneration of RGCs themselves. 92 , 93 Concurrently, immunofluorescence and OCT analyses showed a significant reduction of RGC number and GCL/RNFL thickness in 5xFAD mice retinas compared with WT mice, in accordance with previous findings, 94 , 95 and the presence of active caspase 3 immunostaining in RGCs of 9-month-old 5xFAD mice, suggested RGC death by apoptosis. This is not surprising, because it is widely known that RGCs are heavily affected in different neurodegenerative diseases such as Parkinson’s disease, Huntington's disease, and AD, due to the pathological accumulation of α-synuclein, mutant huntingtin, and different Aβ isoforms, respectively. 96 – 98 APP processing through the amyloidogenic pathway produces different Aβ isoforms. Due to their low solubility, Aβ (1–42) monomers may form high-order assemblies ranging from low molecular weight to midrange- and high-molecular-weight oligomers, fibrils, and senile plaques. Among them, Aβ oligomers seem to be the most toxic form, as they can interact with different types of receptors expressed by neuronal, glial, and endothelial cells, inducing neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, synapse loss, tau protein hyperphosphorylation, and cell death. 99 – 101 In 5xFAD mouse retinas, we identified the presence of extensive Aβ deposits at both 6 and 9 months of age, concurrent with an increase in Aβ (1–42) levels. Although the mechanisms need to be clarified, it has been demonstrated that Aβ may induce neuronal apoptosis both indirectly, triggering neuroinflammatory processes and oxidative stress, or directly altering the levels of some antiapoptotic or proapoptotic factors such as Bcl2 or Bax. 102 , 103 In this respect, our data show higher levels of neuroinflammation and oxidative stress markers, as well as an increased Bax/Bcl2 ratio in 9-month-old 5xFAD mice, together with the presence of active caspase-3 immunoreactive profiles mainly localized in the GCL. Together with neuroinflammation and oxidative stress, we also observed some microgliosis characterized by microglial phenotypical changes. Although this observed microglial activation seemed to be less massive than that reported previously in 5xFAD mice, 104 as well as in the retinas of other mouse models of AD, 105 – 107 it suggested a microglial shift from a surveillant to an activated state. During AD progression, microglial cells may play a dual role, displaying neuroprotective or neurotoxic functions depending on the disease stage and contextual factors. 108 On the one hand, activated microglial cells may cluster around Aβ deposits, acting positively through the formation of a dense barrier that limits Aβ dissemination or by promoting Aβ phagocytosis and protease release. 109 On the other hand, prolonged microglial activation may lead to chronic neuroinflammation, exacerbating neuronal damage. 109 – 111 Indeed, the oligomeric forms of Aβ have been shown to interact with various receptors expressed by microglial cells, among which the receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2, the cluster of differentiation 36 (CD36), CD47, the receptor for advanced glycation end-products, and the α-6/β-1 integrin, initiating a signaling cascade that leads to microglial activation with subsequent Aβ phagocytosis and progressive release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, such as IL-6 and IL-1β. 112 Noteworthy, it has been demonstrated recently that Aβ (1–42) may also interact with the Dectin-1 receptor expressed by microglial cells, inducing activation of the NFκB signaling pathway and subsequent expression of pro-inflammatory factors, such as IL-6. 113 Not surprisingly, we also found an evident increase in NFκB phosphorylation and IL-6 levels in 9-month-old 5xFAD mice. The simultaneous presence of Aβ deposits and reactive microglial cells may cause IPL thickening, as observed in 9-month-old 5xFAD mice, but further evaluation will be necessary. 93 Conversely, no macroglial reactivity was observed in the retina of 5xFAD mice, at variance with previous data reporting an increase of GFAP immunoreactivity. 104 Whereas both microgliosis and macrogliosis are associated with the production of an inflammatory milieu in the AD brain, 114 the presence of activated macroglia in the retina is not so obvious. For instance, different studies reported either increased or decreased GFAP immunoreactivity in the retina of patients with AD as well as in the retina of mouse models of AD. 115 – 119 In addition to Aβ accumulation, we also observed an increase in tau protein phosphorylation in the retinas of both 6- and 9-month-old 5xFAD mice, which is likely to play a key role in retinal degeneration. Normally, the main biological functions of the tau protein are associated with self-assembly and stabilization of the microtubule network. 120 During AD progression, tau protein may undergo hyperphosphorylation in the brain and retinal cells, generating toxic oligomeric forms and NFTs, causing microtubule destabilization with subsequent synaptic dysfunction and neuronal death. 121 Tau protein pathology is closely related to neuroinflammation, considering that sustained and increased neuronal and glial inflammatory responses may exacerbate the tau protein pathology, which further aggravates the inflammatory process. 122 The pTau intracellular accumulation may induce activation of the NFκB pathway and subsequent release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, which, in turn, stimulate microglia activation, leading to a further increase of inflammation and cell death. 123 Overall, our data reveal a sequence of events for the development of retinal pathology in 5xFAD mice in which Aβ deposits and tau protein hyperphosphorylation first appear, at 6 months of age, accompanied by a decline in ERG responses, reduced visual acuity, and RGC death. These observations indicate that the accumulation of Aβ and hyperphosphorylated tau protein may be directly responsible for the retinal deficits observed at 6 months of age in 5xFAD mice. The altered conditions, then, would induce subsequent alterations, such as increased oxidative stress and inflammation, microglia activation, and RGC apoptosis, which have been observed at 9 months of age. According to this temporal pattern, the changes observed at 9 months should be considered as effects, and not causes, of the primary retinal pathology induced by Aβ accumulation and tau protein hyperphosphorylation. In addition, since RGC apoptosis is only detected at 9 months, the decreased RGC number recorded at 6 months is likely to be due to non-apoptotic cell death mechanisms that are typically present in AD, such as ferroptosis, pyroptosis, or necroptosis. 124 , 125 In this regard, necroptotic cell death has been reported recently in RGCs of patients with AD. 126 The present literature indicates that miRNAs play a key role in the regulation of several genes involved in AD. In this regard, murine models represent an effective tool both to validate miRNA targets in vivo and to provide relevant information about the role of specific miRNA-dependent gene pathways in AD. 127 , 128 Numerous studies on murine models of AD identified and validated different dysregulated miRNAs involved in Aβ accumulation, tau protein phosphorylation, neuroinflammation, neuronal apoptosis, neurogenesis, cognitive function, mitochondrial dynamics, and synapse activity. 41 , 127 – 129 Our contribution regarded the identification of 2 dysregulated miRNAs in the 5xFAD mice retinas, miR-3968 and miR-124-5p, both of them becoming downregulated at 9 months. These miRNAs are implicated in biological processes such as apoptosis, inflammation, photoreceptoral differentiation, as well as neuronal and synaptic function. For instance, miR-124 has been identified as a negative regulator of both NF-kB 130 and Bax 131 expression, whereas miR-3968 downregulation has been reported to be associated with inflammatory responses, 132 suggesting that the downregulation of these miRNAs in the 5xFAD mouse retina may be directly linked to increased neuroinflammation and apoptosis. MiR-3968 and miR-124-5p are also known to be correlated to AD, as miR-124 acts by regulating the activity of beta-site amyloid precursor protein cleaving enzyme and subsequent Aβ burden, whereas miR-3968 was associated with the presence of NFT, synaptic dysfunction, and memory loss. 49 , 83 , 133 – 135 Consistent with the present findings, miR-124 has been reported to be downregulated in both in vitro and in vivo models of AD, 49 , 136 whereas miR-3968 was downregulated in the APP/PS1 mouse model. 83 Conclusions AD is the most common form of dementia and, due to the progressive increase of the average age of the world’s population, the number of affected people is expected to grow in the coming years. The 5xFAD mice recapitulate numerous neuropathological signs found in human patients with AD, and the aggressive amyloid pathology they show makes them a good model to study early-onset AD. In a translational perspective, the finding that retinal function and structure are altered in this model indicates that noninvasive analyses, such as ERG and OCT, may help longitudinal monitoring of disease progression in patients with AD from early stages. The present observations also confirm and expand previous data indicating that, besides Aβ formation and tau protein hyperphosphorylation, neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, and microgliosis may play key roles in AD pathogenesis. Moreover, the evidence that miRNA dysregulation in the retina may be related to AD progression opens the field to the possible development of miRNA-based strategies to slow down the disease. Of course, further investigation is required to demonstrate the feasibility of this approach for possible applications in humans.
