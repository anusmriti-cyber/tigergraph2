Abstract Background Alzheimer‘s disease (AD) is the leading cause of cognitive impairment and dementia in elderly patients worldwide. There is increasing evidence that periodontal disease may have an important role in the complex, multifactorial pathogenesis of AD. Aim This narrative review aims to (1) highlight the current understanding of the role of periodontal disease in AD, including molecular and immunological evidence, epidemiological studies, and biological mechanisms linking periodontal disease to AD; and (2) explore the potential impact of periodontal therapy as part of an individualized, multitherapeutic approach to AD. Materials and methods A literature search of the PubMed database was conducted using Boolean search strategies to identify publications related to the potential connections between periodontal disease and AD. Results Most of the evidence for a link between periodontal disease and AD is limited to preclinical research and epidemiological investigations. A direct causal link has not yet been demonstrated in human clinical studies, but periodontal pathogenic bacteria have been detected in brain tissue and cerebrospinal fluid of patients with AD. Further, colocalization of gingipain proteases secreted by Porphyromonas gingivalis has been found in AD pathological lesions. Epidemiological studies support associations between periodontal disease and increased risk/prevalence of cognitive decline, AD, and AD mortality. Two mechanistic theories have been proposed to explain the connection between periodontitis and AD: the “microbial involvement” theory focuses on periodontal disease–associated pathogenic bacteria, whereas the “inflammatory cascade” theory focuses on proinflammatory mediators as drivers of neuroinflammation that may exacerbate pathologic lesions associated with AD. Preclinical studies of periodontal therapies targeting oral microbiota or their byproducts have investigated small‐molecule gingipain inhibitors and novel therapeutics that restore oral microbial homeostasis (e.g., probiotic bacteriocin nisin). In animal models, gingipain inhibitors and nisin showed inhibitory effects on formation of pathological lesions of AD or neuroinflammation and microbiome changes, respectively; however, no impact on cognition was found with use of gingipain inhibitors in patients with mild‐to‐moderate AD. Conclusions Additional studies are needed to better understand the potential causal relationship between periodontal disease and AD, including further exploration of therapies targeting the oral–brain axis. The role of the oral microbiome, host response, and periodontal disease treatment in Alzheimer's disease: A primer . Periodontology 2000 . 2025 ; 98 : 220 ‐ 227 . doi: 10.1111/prd.12631 PMC12842860 40495582 1 INTRODUCTION During the past several decades, there has been increasing interest in the potential connection between periodontal disease and systemic conditions, including neurodegenerative diseases. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 Among these, Alzheimer's disease (AD) is a leading cause of cognitive impairment globally, 5 and research has suggested that chronic periodontal infection and related inflammation can negatively affect the central nervous system (CNS) and predispose to cognitive impairment. 1 , 2 , 6 , 7 , 8 Two key features define AD: (1) excessive production of insoluble, fibrillary extracellular amyloid‐β plaques in the brain, which are known to cause neuronal damage; and (2) accumulation of hyperphosphorylated tau‐positive neurofibrillary tangles, which disrupt synaptic connections and neuronal communications and lead to cognitive function decline. 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 However, triggers of these pathological processes in AD are unclear. 9 , 10 A classic hypothesis involves the role of altered metabolism and processing of amyloid precursor protein (APP), resulting in abnormal accumulation of amyloid‐β alongside tau tangle development. 13 , 14 This hypothesis is based on studies of the familial or early‐onset form of AD, which has identifiable APP gene mutations. 13 , 14 For the sporadic or late‐onset form, which accounts for &gt;95% of AD cases, 14 , 15 the etiology of amyloid deposition is less clear, and causation is considered multifactorial. 16 Age, medical comorbidities (e.g., vascular disease and traumatic brain injury), lifestyle choices (e.g., physical activity and social engagement levels), and environmental factors can influence the risk for development of late‐onset AD. 16 In addition, genetic susceptibility plays an important role. 16 The estimated heritability rate of late‐onset AD is 60%–80%, 16 and the strongest risk occurs in individuals with the ε4 allele of apolipoprotein E. 16 An inflammatory hypothesis has also been formulated to explain the pathogenesis of AD. 2 It has been suggested that amyloid‐β senile plaques and tau neurofibrillary tangles increase the production of proinflammatory cytokines, such as tumor necrosis factor‐α (TNF‐α), interleukin‐1β (IL‐1β), and interleukin‐6 (IL‐6), which, in turn, further activate amyloid‐β synthesis and tau phosphorylation, thus maintaining a chronic cyclic inflammatory response in the CNS. 2 In addition, amyloid‐β senile plaques can activate immune cells in the brain (microglia), which release IL‐1β and cathepsin B and induce prolonged neuroinflammation that worsens neuronal damage and accelerates AD progression. 6 , 17 , 18 In addition to this localized inflammation in the CNS, peripheral inflammation, such as that caused by periodontitis‐related gram‐negative bacteria, may also contribute to the pathogenesis of late‐onset AD. 2 , 19 Oral dysbiosis‐induced inflammation and associated accumulation of amyloid‐β plaques may also contribute to cognitive impairment in early‐stage AD. 20 , 21 Pathogens from the periphery (e.g., oral microbes) may directly activate inflammatory processes in the brain by crossing the blood–brain barrier or may indirectly increase cerebral inflammation via increases in circulating proinflammatory mediators, thereby activating amyloid production. 2 , 19 , 22 Given the complexity and multifactorial nature of AD pathogenesis, an individualized, multitherapeutic approach is needed to address potential contributors to disease onset. 23 This narrative review aims to (1) highlight the current understanding of the role of periodontal disease in AD, including molecular and immunological evidence, epidemiological studies, and biological mechanisms linking periodontal disease to AD; and (2) explore the potential impact of periodontal therapy as part of an individualized, multitherapeutic approach to AD. A literature search of the PubMed database was conducted using a Boolean strategy to identify English‐language publications related to the potential connections between periodontal disease and AD. 2 MOLECULAR AND IMMUNOLOGICAL EVIDENCE A potential link between periodontal health and neurologic health was initially suggested based on the detection of Treponema , a bacterium commonly found in the oral cavity and in harvested brain tissues, including the trigeminal ganglia, brainstem, and cortex, of patients with AD. 24 It was proposed that Treponema from the oral cavity might reach the brain through branches of the trigeminal nerve. 24 Additional oral bacterial species have also been implicated in neurologic health and the pathogenesis of AD, including Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans , 25 Fusobacterium nucleatum , 26 Prevotella intermedia , 26 Actinomyces naeslundii , 27 and Porphyromonas gingivalis . 28 Serotype b of A. actinomycetemcomitans , associated with severe forms of periodontal disease, has been shown to trigger the secretion of proinflammatory cytokines and amyloid‐β peptide in hippocampal cell cultures. 25 Serum levels of antibodies to F. nucleatum and P. intermedia were found to be elevated in patients with AD, years before a clinical diagnosis of cognitive impairment. 26 High serum levels of A. naeslundii were associated with an increased risk for developing incident AD in a case cohort study. 27 Most of the evidence for a potential connection with AD pathology has come from animal and human studies of P. gingivalis , the keystone pathogen in periodontal disease. 2 , 29 Animal models of periodontitis have shown that P. gingivalis accesses the brain, contributes to complement activation, and exacerbates brain amyloid‐β deposition. 21 , 30 In humans with AD, P. gingivalis DNA was found in brain tissue and living‐donor cerebrospinal fluid samples, and immunoreactivity to gingipains (proteases secreted by P. gingivalis ) was found in brain tissue samples; gingipains were also colocalized with neurons, tau tangles, and amyloid‐β in the hippocampus. 28 , 31 , 32 Of note, oxidative stress is an important contributor to inflammation 33 and has been implicated in the pathogenesis of both periodontitis and AD. 34 , 35 , 36 Collectively, these observations support a possible relationship between periodontal disease and progression of AD via inflammation caused directly or indirectly by periodontal bacteria. 37 , 38 3 EPIDEMIOLOGICAL STUDIES Epidemiological research has provided further evidence of the connection between periodontitis and the risk of AD. A retrospective, matched‐cohort study showed that patients aged ≥50 years had a 1.7‐fold greater risk of developing AD after 10 years of chronic periodontitis exposure compared with patients without chronic periodontitis. 39 In the United States, metrics indicative of compromised oral health relevant to periodontal disease were positively correlated with the prevalence and mortality of AD, as well as the prevalence of subjective cognitive decline. 40 A population‐level association with AD has also been found for periodontal pathogenic bacteria. 41 , 42 A retrospective study using the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) III linked with Medicare data demonstrated that serum immunoglobulin G titers against several different periodontal pathogens, including P. gingivalis , were associated with an increased risk for incident AD‐associated dementia and AD‐related mortality among US elderly patients aged ≥65 years. 41 Another analysis of NHANES III linked with Medicare data found that seropositivity for P. gingivalis and Streptococcus oralis at higher infection burdens was associated with a higher risk of AD. 43 Other population studies have shown that oral bacterial loads of F. nucleatum were significantly higher in patients with AD compared with controls. 42 , 44 These studies have focused on the association between periodontal disease or its pathogens and AD. 39 , 41 , 42 A retrospective cohort study went a step further and examined the link between periodontal disease treatment and mortality in patients with dementia. 45 In that study, which monitored patients during 17 years of follow‐up, the risk of death in patients with AD‐related dementia who did not receive periodontal treatment was 1.8 times higher than that in patients who received periodontal treatment. 45 These findings support the importance of maintaining oral health in patients with AD. 4 BIOLOGICAL MECHANISMS LINKING PERIODONTAL DISEASE AND ALZHEIMER'S DISEASE Data to date suggest a correlation between periodontal disease and AD, but it is more complex to determine a cause‐and‐effect association. 1 , 46 Animal models of infection‐induced periodontal disease show the development of pathological hallmark lesions of AD, including extracellular amyloid‐β plaques and phosphorylated tau protein bound to neurofibrillary tangles in the hippocampus, as well as a clinical phenotype of impaired spatial learning and memory. 47 , 48 These results suggest a causative role of periodontal infection in the development of AD lesions and cognitive impairment. 47 However, data from human studies are less conclusive. 46 A 2022 meta‐analysis confirmed a moderate association of periodontitis with age‐related cognitive impairment (particularly dementia and AD) in humans, but causality could not be inferred because of data heterogeneity and risk of trial bias. 1 If periodontal disease is involved in the pathogenesis of AD, the next question is what role periodontal pathogens and inflammation may play and how they might contribute to brain inflammation mechanistically. Theoretically, two possible mechanisms may connect periodontal disease and AD: a “microbial involvement mechanism” and an “inflammatory cascade mechanism” (Figure 1 ). 6 In the microbial involvement mechanism, invasive and virulent periodontal pathogens, such as P. gingivalis and Treponema , induce gingival inflammation, degrade connective tissue, and resorb alveolar bone around teeth. 30 During this process, (1) the junctional epithelium linking the gingiva to the tooth enamel is transformed into pocket epithelium with a reservoir of pathogenic bacteria, leading to bacteremia via infiltration of local blood vessels (i.e., leaky gums), or (2) bacteria travel directly to the brain via neuronal transport; both processes promote cytokine release and amyloid deposition. 6 , 30 In the inflammatory cascade mechanism, periodontal bacteria do not physically spread through the CNS, but rather inflammatory mediators (e.g., C‐reactive protein, IL‐1, IL‐6, TNF‐α, and α‐1‐antichymotrypsin) originating from periodontal disease, via the leaky gums, increase systemic inflammation, including in the brain. This increases production of amyloid‐β and tau hyperphosphorylation and exacerbates the neurodegenerative process of AD. 6 FIGURE 1 Mechanism of microbial involvement or inflammatory cascade potentially linking periodontal disease and Alzheimer's disease. In the “microbial involvement mechanism,” periodontal pathogenic bacteria or their byproducts migrate from the leaky gums to the brain via bloodstream and/or neuronal transport and cause neuroinflammation, cytokine release, and amyloid deposition. 6 , 30 In the “inflammatory cascade mechanism,” proinflammatory mediators due to periodontal disease increase systemic inflammation and promote neuroinflammation and amyloid deposition in the brain. 6 Adapted with permission from figure by Ryutaro Kuraji and Yvonne Kapila, Copyright © 2024. Studies of gingipains support the plausibility of these biological mechanisms. In animal and cell culture studies, gingipains induced endothelial cell detachment, apoptosis, and enhanced vascular permeability, 49 , 50 implicating their role in breaking down tight junctions of the blood–brain barrier and providing an entry route for microorganisms, with induction of inflammatory mediators. 51 Gingipains increased gene expression of APP and APP cleaving enzyme, production of amyloid‐β and tau tangles, and loss of neuronal synapses, thus connecting this P. gingivalis virulence factor with AD pathology. 21 , 52 , 53 Gingipains also enhanced activation and migration of microglia toward the P. gingivalis‐ infected sites and induced neuroinflammation and release of inflammatory mediators (IL‐1β, IL‐6, and TNF‐α) in animal models. 51 , 54 In brain tissues of patients with AD, as mentioned earlier, gingipains were found at higher levels than in control brains and were associated with the hallmark signs of tau tangles and amyloid‐β. 28 , 31 , 32 Furthermore, genes differentially expressed in mouse and human brain tissues with detected P. gingivalis sequencing reads, as well as genes associated with gingipain susceptibility, were highly expressed in the anterior hypothalamus, cholinergic neurons, and the basal forebrain regions, 55 thereby associating P. gingivalis and gingipains with the brain areas involved early in the disease process of AD. It is possible that a bidirectional relationship could exist between periodontitis and AD (oral cavity−brain axis), similar to the concept of the gut–brain axis, a bidirectional communication between the gastrointestinal tract microbiota and the CNS. 56 , 57 , 58 , 59 Studies conducted mostly on animal models suggest that the pathways of communication in the gut–brain axis include the autonomic nervous system, the neuroendocrine system, and the immune system via portal circulation. 56 , 58 Within the gastrointestinal tract, a variety of microbial‐derived molecules, including hormones, neurotransmitters, and metabolites (e.g., short‐chain fatty acids), are implicated in the pathogenesis of neurodegenerative diseases including AD. 57 Conversely, nervous system components, such as stress hormones, immune mediators, and CNS neurotransmitters, are believed to activate the enteric nervous system and modulate the gut microbiota. 56 This bidirectional relationship and impact of the gut microbiota in the gut−brain axis will likely help guide future studies related to an oral cavity−brain axis. 5 PERIODONTAL THERAPIES AND IMPACT ON COGNITION Standard non‐pharmacologic approaches to periodontitis include daily oral hygiene measures complemented by professional removal of biofilms during supportive periodontal care. 60 Addressing inflammation is likely to be an important component of patient management, given it plays a key role in the pathogenesis of both periodontal disease and AD. Epidemiologic studies have shown that the use of nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) may modify cognitive trajectories and prevent cognitive decline if started in midlife (before 65 years of age), years before the occurrence of AD‐related dementia. 61 Clinical trials have evaluated the neuronal effects of various NSAIDs in patients with AD but have produced mixed results. In a trial of indomethacin 100–150 mg/day ( N = 66), cognitive decline was slowed compared with placebo treatment in patients with AD ( p &lt; 0.003). 62 However, a trial of rofecoxib or naproxen failed to demonstrate a beneficial effect versus placebo on AD progression, 63 suggesting that benefit with NSAIDs is not a class effect. 64 Although no clinical trials have specifically evaluated the neuronal effects of periodontal anti‐inflammatory agents, a prospective study of patients with gingivitis/periodontitis and mild AD showed that dental treatment could reduce gingival inflammation and significantly improve cognitive parameters by 6 months after the dental intervention. 65 These results indicate the potential importance of inhibiting periodontal inflammation for maintaining cognitive function. Given the association of gingipains and periodontal pathogens with the biological features of AD, there has been interest in evaluating how therapeutics targeting the oral microbiota might affect cognitive outcomes. 31 , 51 , 66 For example, administration of small‐molecule gingipain inhibitors (in animal models or in neuronal cell cultures infected with P. gingivalis or exposed to gingipains) decreased the P. gingivalis bacterial load in the brain, reduced the secretion of inflammatory mediators and neurotoxicity of gingipains, and reduced the formation of amyloid plaques and tau tangles. 31 , 51 The gingipain inhibitor atuzaginstat was evaluated in a phase 2/3 clinical trial as a potential inhibitor of this bacterial virulence factor in patients with mild‐to‐moderate AD ( NCT03823404 ); however, the study failed to detect any differences versus placebo in cognitive parameters after 48 weeks of treatment. 51 , 67 To date, other gingipain inhibitors have not been evaluated in human trials. Therapeutics that restore the balance of the oral microbiome have been proposed as potential treatments for oral and systemic diseases. Nisin, an antimicrobial peptide (lantibiotic) produced by the probiotic Lactococcus lactis , has shown efficacy in modulating oral microbial dysbiosis toward health and attenuating the systemic host immune response. 68 , 69 Research on oral biofilms has indicated that both L. lactis and nisin prevent biofilm formation, disrupt preformed biofilms, decrease oral pathogen load within biofilms, and can restore microbial diversity back to control levels. 70 In addition, preclinical studies support immunomodulatory effects of nisin via induction of chemokine secretion and suppression of lipopolysaccharide‐stimulated cytokines. 71 , 72 In an animal model of periodontal infection, oral application of nisin affected several components within the brain: It modulated the composition of bacteria, reduced DNA levels of periodontal pathogens, decreased mRNA expression of proinflammatory cytokines, and decreased the deposition of amyloid‐β and phosphorylated tau. 66 Probiotics may be appropriate as part of a prophylactic or treatment regimen for periodontitis and gingivitis, given their modulation of the oral microbiota and positive impact on the host immune response. 73 , 74 , 75 , 76 Oral microbiota transplantation (OMT) and natural compounds, such as quercetin, a plant‐derived dietary polyphenol, are novel therapeutics that modulate oral microbial dysbiosis in preclinical studies and have potential as adjunctive therapies for periodontitis. 77 , 78 An investigation of OMT for periodontitis in canines found that, compared with chemical plus mechanical full‐mouth debridement, receipt of a healthy donor OMT as an adjunct to debridement led to a notable, albeit transient (&lt;12 weeks), “ecological shift” toward the donor microbiota composition. 79 An exploratory clinical trial in 16 adults with severe periodontitis confirmed the presence of distinct oral microbial signatures for periodontitis versus a healthy oral cavity, supporting the rationale for investing in OMT as a potential therapeutic approach for humans. 80 In a murine periodontitis model, oral administration of quercetin helped maintain oral microbiome diversity. 78 Additionally, in human macrophages, quercetin treatment was associated with anti‐inflammatory effects, including reduced TNF and IL‐6 production, following infection with P. gingivalis and F. nucleatum . Other than nisin, these additional approaches (OMT, quercetin) have not been evaluated for their impact on the CNS via modulation of the oral microbiota, but further investigation in neurodegenerative diseases is warranted. 6 FUTURE DIRECTIONS Extensive research has been dedicated to the etiology and pathogenesis of cognitive impairment, and the multifactorial nature of neurocognitive decline complicates approaches for risk reduction and targeted therapeutics and requires a multimodal approach. The relationship between periodontal disease and AD at the molecular, immunological, and population levels supports a role of effectively managing periodontal health to maintain healthy cognitive function as individuals age. For patients at high risk of AD, a more intense periodontal health maintenance regimen may be advised. In addition, routine fluid testing (e.g., salivary) for periodontal pathogens in selected patients may allow early identification and treatment of at‐risk patients with gingivitis/periodontitis and therefore limit its systemic impact. The prevention and treatment of periodontal disease can be easily incorporated into the multitherapeutic and integrative approach to address multiple potential contributors to cognitive decline in patients with AD. It is important to acknowledge that a causal relationship between periodontal disease and AD has not been established. More definitive evidence may come from clinical studies of periodontal therapies and their effects on the oral and brain microbiomes and on clinical parameters of cognition. 7 CLINICAL RELEVANCE AND FUTURE OUTLOOK Most of the evidence for a link between periodontal disease and AD has come from preclinical research and epidemiological investigations, whereas a direct causal link has not yet been demonstrated in human clinical studies. Given that a connection between periodontal disease and AD is emerging and because periodontal infection/inflammation is a modifiable risk factor (i.e., periodontal treatment can be easily incorporated into a multitherapeutic strategy), employing a multitherapeutic strategy and tailoring treatments to new disease connections/mechanisms hold promise for improving AD outcomes. Based on preliminary research, further exploration of therapies targeting the proposed microbial involvement or inflammatory cascade mechanisms in the oral–brain axis is warranted.
