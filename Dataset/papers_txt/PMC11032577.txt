Abstract INTRODUCTION Rate of cognitive decline (RCD) in Alzheimer's disease (AD) determines the degree of impairment for patients and of burden for caretakers. We studied the association of RCD with genetic variants in AD. METHODS RCD was evaluated in 62 familial AD (FAD) and 53 sporadic AD (SAD) cases, and analyzed by whole‐exome sequencing for association with common exonic functional variants. Findings were validated in post mortem brain tissue. RESULTS One hundred seventy‐two gene variants in FAD, and 227 gene variants in SAD associated with RCD. In FAD, performance decline of the immediate recall of the Rey‐Osterrieth figure test associated with 122 genetic variants. Olfactory receptor OR51B6 showed the highest number of associated variants. Its expression was detected in temporal cortex neurons. DISCUSSION Impaired olfactory function has been associated with cognitive impairment in AD. Genetic variants in these or other genes could help to identify risk of faster memory decline in FAD and SAD patients. All author affiliations have been renumbered accordingly.] 1 BACKGROUND Alzheimer's disease (AD) is the main cause of dementia with ≈ 40 million affected individuals worldwide. 1 The great majority of AD cases are of sporadic nature, and &lt; 1% of AD cases present with autosomal dominant inheritance with mutations in APP , PSEN1 , and PSEN2 genes, leading to familial AD (FAD). From a clinical point of view, FAD is mainly distinguished from sporadic AD (SAD) by its earlier age of onset, while other clinical features including dementia duration and cognitive decline,+ are fairly similar between both types of AD. 2 , 3 In PSEN1 mutation–associated FAD, cognitive decline starts with memory complaints ≈ 11 years before dementia, leading to amnestic mild cognitive impairment (MCI), and eventually to dementia. 4 In sporadic cases, not all MCI–affected individuals develop AD dementia, 5 but once dementia starts, it generally follows a characteristic clinical course that would lead to death within 10 years in average. 3 , 6 Memory loss is the main cognitive feature in AD clinical progression. 2 However, other cognitive domains such as language and executive function have shown to be also severely impaired in AD. 7 The rate of progression of cognitive decline in AD, considered the speed at which cognitive decline occurs, has shown to be variable in SAD, 8 and less so in FAD. 9 In both cases, rate of decline can be affected by other factors such as years of education 10 or apolipoprotein E ( APOE ) haplotype. 11 , 12 Not all cognitive domains decline at the same rate, and more rapidly declining domains can be disease specific, with memory, language, and executive function showing faster decline rates in AD. 13 Some attempts have been made to find genetic markers for cognitive decline progression in AD, with variable success. 14 , 15 Some known markers for AD risk have been identified as associating with more severe cognitive decline using whole cognitive test batteries, 16 , 17 but so far not even APOE ε4 has shown to have a clear impact in the progression of cognitive decline. 18 During the last 30 years we have been successfully characterizing the largest pedigree carrying a single mutation (E280A) in the PSEN1 gene and suffering from FAD. This characterization includes long‐term clinical follow‐up, cognitive 3 , 4 and neuropathological characterization, 19 , 20 together with genetic and genomic studies for specific endophenotypes such as age of disease onset. 21 , 22 , 23 This population is ideally suited for association studies given sample size and the wealth of clinical data collected during &gt; 20 years of follow‐up. Longitudinal studies in this population identified ages of onset and rate of progression based on cognitive tests, detecting a median age of MCI and dementia at 44 and 49 years old, respectively. 4 A subsequent study determined that in the majority of carriers, the recall of the word list was the earliest test to determine the preclinical onset of the disease at 32 years of age. 24 In this study, we asked if individuals who showed faster cognitive decline on cognitive tests had differences in common genetic variants compared to individuals who did not progress as quickly, and if these genetic variants could be associated with rapid progression of specific cognitive domains. We considered this likely given the current record of genetic variants with high impact as disease modifiers in the PSEN1 E280A FAD population. 25 , 26 In this study, we take advantage of whole‐exome sequencing (WES) data and of cognitive follow‐up data collected from a subset of PSEN1 E280A FAD and SAD patients, and expand our previous association studies to identify genetic variant associated with rate of cognitive decline (RCD) in specific cognitive domains and tests. We identified genes associated with accelerated RCD, particularly memory function and specific memory cognitive tests. RESEARCH IN CONTEXT Systematic review : A review was carried out in PubMed identifying possible genetic variants associated with the severity of the disease. The search terms were: (rate cognitive progression) AND (((((progression) OR (prognosis)) OR (disease progression)) AND (genetic variants)) AND (Alzheimer's disease)). Thirty‐seven results were returned. Although the genetic variants associated with Alzheimer's disease (AD) have been studied, the genetic variants associated with the rate of cognitive progression are not known with certainty. Interpretation : Our findings contribute to generating new hypotheses regarding olfactory receptors and their possible roles in AD pathophysiology. Future directions : This study, and other evidence references in the manuscript, show that olfactory receptors may act as modifiers of AD progression, and may be pivotal to better understanding cognition from a biological and functional perspective. 2 METHODS 2.1 Patients All participants of the study signed informed consent for clinical evaluations and genetic assessment, according to international regulations and approved by the ethical board of the School of Medicine of the University of Antioquia, Medellín, Colombia. As a first selection criterion we included a subset of patients carrying the PSEN1 E280A mutation with clinical and neuropsychological follow‐up, composed by several evaluations from MCI up to late stages of dementia. Detailed clinical assessment and evaluation of these patients is described elsewhere; 4 in brief, each patient underwent medical, neurological, and neuropsychological evaluations as often as necessary during the course of their clinical follow‐up until their death or closure of the study. For cognitive assessment, we used the Spanish version of The Consortium to Establish a Registry for Alzheimer's disease (CERAD) evaluation battery, 27 adapted for the cultural and linguistic characteristics specific to this population as previously described. 9 , 28 The second selection criterion was to include patients with the PSEN1 E280A mutation that had WES data available (see genotyping methods) and met the first selection criteria. Using these two criteria, we studied 62 PSEN1 E280A patients with an average of 3.8 evaluations (Table S1 in supporting information). We also studied 53 SAD cases that fulfilled the same selection criteria. Some of these SAD patients belong also to the PSEN1 E280A genealogies but were tested as non‐carriers for the mutation and eventually developed SAD. Given that SAD patients are only identified after the onset of dementia, they presented a significantly lower average number of neurological and neuropsychological evaluations that started at a later stage of dementia (Table S1 ). As these SAD cases presented with AD dementia, cognitive decline, and share the same genetic background and genealogy as the population with the E280A mutation, they were used as a control population in this study. 2.2 Assessment of cognitive domains and progression of decline The CERAD battery includes different tests that can be grouped according to different cognitive domains (memory, language, executive function, attention, and praxis; Table S2 in supporting information). We have used this grouping method previously to assess cognitive decline in different cognitive domains from asymptomatic to demented PSEN1 E280A carriers. Briefly, the value obtained in each test was normalized with a Z score and averaged with other tests belonging to a particular cognitive domain, as indicated 4 (Table S2 ). For the present study, we selected only language, memory, praxis, attention, and executive function domains because they include more than one neuropsychological test (Table S2 ). Normalized values from each neuropsychological test and cognitive domains were considered independent variables for genetic association analysis. Missing data, a common feature of clinical studies, were handled using the imputation method implemented in the “missForest” 29 , 30 package for R. 31 Subsequent statistical analyses were performed on the imputed data set. Another factor to be considered regarding cognitive decline progression is that FAD and SAD patients show variable ages of onset, and not all patients started to be evaluated at the same stage of disease. Therefore, cognitive domains decline does not show a uniform pattern in these populations (Figure S1A, B in supporting information), unless the stage of dementia is assessed for each evaluation point. We combined the Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE) 32 and the Global Deterioration Scale (GDS) 33 for determining disease progression in our samples. Both scales are generally applied in our service and each one of them brings a general view, whether of cognitive status with the MMSE, or about the general status of the patient at a given moment with daily living assessment using the GDS. By combining both scales, we established a cognitive decline progression scale (Table 1 ; Figure 1A ) that not only reflects cognitive decline in the patients (Figure S2 in supporting information), but also allows us to normalize cognitive decline, independent of age or point of entry into cognitive assessment, for all evaluated cognitive domains (Figure 1B ). AD, Alzheimer's disease; CoDR, Cognitive Decline Rank; FAD, familial Alzheimer's disease; SAD, sporadic Alzheimer's disease. 2.3 Genotyping 2.3.1 Whole‐exome sequencing One hundred fifteen individuals (62 with FAD from the E280A pedigree and 53 individuals with SAD) were whole‐exome genotyped using Illumina HumanExome BeadChip‐12v1_A. This single nucleotide polymorphism (*SNP) chip covers putative functional exonic variants selected from &gt; 12,000 individual exome and whole‐genome sequences and consists of ≈ 250,000 markers representing diverse populations (including European, African, Chinese, and Hispanic individuals) and a range of common conditions such as type 2 diabetes, cancer, and metabolic and psychiatric disorders. In addition to pure exonic variation, the HumanExome BeadChip‐12v1_A chip covers SNPs in splice sites, in stop variants, in promoter regions, genome‐wide association study tag markers, among other potentially functional variations. Samples with calls below Illumina's expected 99% SNP call rates were excluded. 2.3.2 Whole‐exome capture We performed also whole‐exome capture (WEC) on 14 individuals with AD from the E280A sample. Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood from all patients and processed by the Australian Genome Facility. DNA libraries were constructed from 1 μg of genomic DNA using an Illumina TruSeq genomic DNA library kit (Illumina Inc.), and libraries were multiplexed with six samples pooled together (500 ng each). Exons were enriched from 3 μg of pooled library DNA using an Illumina TruSeq Exome enrichment kit (Illumina Inc.), and ran on a 100‐base‐pair paired‐end run on an Illumina HiSeq 2000 sequencer (Illumina Inc.). A total of 201,071 genomic regions (sampled at ≈ 50X coverage) were surveyed using the WEC platform. Subsequently, sequencing image data were processed in real time using Illumina's Real Time Analysis software and converted to FASTQ files using the CASAVA pipeline (Illumina Inc.). The resulting FASTQ files were further processed for variant analysis using Golden Helix's SNP variation suite (SVS) 8.3.0 (Golden Helix, Inc.). 2.4 Genetic, statistical, and bioinformatics analysis 2.4.1 Quality control, filtering, and classification of functional variants Genotypes for all individuals from the PSEN1 E280A pedigree and SAD cases were obtained, and quality control subsequently performed using the following criteria: (1) deviations from Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium with P values &lt; 0.05/m (where m is the number of markers included for analysis), (2) a minimum genotype call rate of 90%, (3) the presence of more than two alleles, and (4) monoallelic markers. Markers meeting any of these criteria were excluded from analyses. Genotype and allelic frequencies were estimated by maximum likelihood. To define the presence of population stratification between these cohorts we explored the effect of genetic differentiation between and within (populations and individuals, respectively) these cohorts and found non‐significant deviations from expectations of the Fst parameter (Fst for these cohorts was 0.0187). 22 Considering that Fst values &gt; 0.17 suggest potential stratification between cohorts, we can confidently say these cohorts are unrelated. In addition, we also estimated kinship relatedness, that is, pairs of FAD and SAD cases with Fst values &gt; 0.025 were discarded. Following previous recommendations, 34 variants with a minor allele frequency (MAF) ≥ 0.01 were classified as common and as rare otherwise. Exonic variants with potential functional effect were determined using the functional prediction information available in the dbNSFP_NS_Functional_Predictions GRCh_37 annotation track, which uses SIFT, PolyPhen‐2, Mutation Taster, Gerp++, and PhyloP19‐21 and is fully implemented in the Golden Helix SVS 8.9.1 Variant Classification module. 35 , 36 , 37 , 38 , 39 This module was also used to examine interactions between variants and gene transcripts to classify variants based on their potential effect on genes. For filtering of variants in the FAD cohort, Filter 1 included common and uncommon variants captured by the whole‐exome sequencing. Filter 2 excluded variants with a genotype call rate &gt; 90% in Hardy–Weinberg disequilibrium and with one or more than two alleles. Filter 3 excluded variants with MAF &lt; 1% of the functional effect. Therefore, in FAD, Filter 1 resulted in 242,545 variants and Filter 2 in 49,187. After Filter 3, 39,753 variants remained for analysis. 22 For the SAD cohort submitted to quality control, Filter 1 excluded variants with a genotype call rate &lt; 90%, in Hardy–Weinberg disequilibrium and with one or more than two alleles and with a MAF &lt; 1%, and Filter 2 excluded those not functional. After Filter 1, 38,157 variants remained for analysis, while after applying Filter 2, 11,544 remained. 40 In all these processes, pruning was applied, and redundant common markers were used for the estimation of relatedness among individuals to control mixed models of association. Variants were classified according to their position in a gene transcript. In addition, variants in coding exons were further classified according to their effect on the gene's protein sequence. 2.4.2 Exonic variants modifying rate of cognitive decline Because of the longitudinal nature of the clinical assessment, linear mixed‐effects models (LMEMs) 41 were fitted to test for associations between common exonic functional variants (CEFVs) and our clinical phenotypes. For a given cognitive domain, a LMEM was initially fitted using sex, years of education, and age at the time of clinical assessment as fixed effects, and individuals as random effects (i.e., the null model). Subsequently, each CEFV was independently included as part of the fixed effects and a new LMEM was fitted (i.e., the alternative model). The contribution of each CEFV to model fitting was determined using a likelihood ratio test (LRT), which expresses how many times more likely the data are under the alternative than on the null model. 42 Variants for which the LRT disclosed a significant contribution were considered to potentially contribute to clinical performance in our sample based on the specific clinical measure. Model fitting and testing was implemented in R using the lmer4 43 package. Within each phenotype, P values were corrected for multiple testing using the false discovery rate (FDR) 44 and a method based on extremes‐value theory. 45 2.4.3 Gene‐based association tests Given the presence of genes with more than one variant associating with cognitive variables, we performed gene‐based analysis using Versatile Gene‐Based Test for Genome‐wide Association Studies version 2 (VEGAS2) 46 under the default settings in our AD groups. VEGAS2 is a freely available software to perform gene‐based association analysis. As implemented, VEGAS2 produces a gene‐based test statistic and an empirical gene‐based P value obtained using a simulation‐based approach while correcting by linkage disequilibrium (LD) patterns for each gene. By default, LD patterns are estimated using the HapMap2 Centre d'Etude du Polymorphism Humain/Utah population. SNPs and/or genes shown to be statistically significant using VEGAS2 were classified as cognitive decline modifiers in AD. 2.5 Histological analysis 2.5.1 In situ hybridization Human brain tissue from healthy controls without evidence of neurodegeneration and donated to the brain bank of the University of Antioquia after informed consent signature by next of kin was obtained, and in agreement with local ethical laws, were fixed with 4% buffered formalin and processed for paraffin embedding. Sections (3 μm) were subjected to in situ hybridization. OR51B6 RNA was detected in formalin‐fixed paraffin‐embedded brain tissue with formalin fixation time of &lt; 48 hours with the RNAscope 2.5 HD Assay and Detection Reagent Kit Red (Advanced Cell Diagnostics [ACD]) following the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, brain sections were deparaffinized, incubated with the hybridization probe, enhanced with amplification buffer, washed several times, and detected with Fast Red substrate before counterstaining with hematoxylin. As positive and negative control probes, we used ACD's Hs‐PPIB or DapB probes, respectively. We used a custom‐made probe produced and validated by ACD to detect OR51B6 RNA. 3 RESULTS 3.1 Clinical characterization of individuals with FAD and SAD We have reported previously the cognitive decline progress profile in PSEN1 E280A patients according to cognitive domains evaluated in the CERAD battery test 4 (Table S1 ). However, when analyzing the impact of genetic variation in cognitive decline in AD, two aspects should be considered. First, even among PSEN1 E280A FAD patients there is a wide spread of age of onset. 4 Second, the cognitive decline of AD patients as evaluated on a first examination can vary depending on the stage of deterioration of each patient at that point (Figure S1A ). To uniformize cognitive decline in FAD and SAD patients for association analyses, controlling for age of onset of symptoms and accounting for the introduction of patients at different stage of decline to the follow‐up analysis, we stratified them using the MMSE as a direct overview of cognitive impairment and the GDS for assessing dementia severity progression. By combining the scales, we obtained a stepwise assessment of cognitive decline (Table 1 ). The GDS provided differentiation between patients otherwise judged to be similar according to the MMSE. The application of this Cognitive Decline Rank (CoDR) allowed us to successfully stratify the progression of cognitive decline in our AD patients in all assessed domains, independently of age of onset (Figure 1A ; Figure S1B‐C ). In general, FAD showed a clearer progression in decline compared to SAD patients, particularly for attention and executive function domains (Figure 1B ). 3.2 Genetic association analyses For genetic association studies in both FAD and SAD patients we took 28 variables, corresponding to 23 outputs from 12 tests and their resulting cognitive domains. For each variable a Z score was calculated, and its progression during the longitudinal follow‐up of the patients (Figures S2‐S3 in supporting information) was calculated as a slope (Figure S4 in supporting information) that was used as the final variable for genetic association analyses. A total of 11 variables showed statistically significant association hits in both groups. Memory was the only cognitive domain showing positive association results in all tests and variables. Six out of eleven variables correspond to three memory‐related tests (i.e., the word list test, line recall test, and Rey–Osterrieth Complex Figure [R‐OFR] Immediate Recall). The highest significant associations after FDR and Bonferroni corrections for multiple testing were identified for R‐OFR and the memory domain (Table 2 , Figure 2A ). We replicated these analyses in our SAD sample (Table S5 in supporting information) and identified 227 gene variants that were associated with 11 cognitive variables, 10 of them already identified in the FAD (Table 2 , Data file S 2 and Figure S6 ). Some differences in genetic association and cognitive decline between FAD and SAD became apparent. Although many genetic markers were associated to R‐OFR in our SAD sample, this is not the variable showing the largest number of associations. In fact, 72 genetic variants were found to be associated with the number of intrusions in the word list test (Table S5 ), followed by the executive function domain, for which we found that 50 genetic markers were associated with the fast total test (Table 2 ). Even though the cognitive variables showing genetic association were similar between FAD and SAD, we were not able to identify a gene or gene variant associated in both groups. Nevertheless, there were families of proteins commonly associated with decline in specific cognitive tests (Table S4 ). Among the wide assortment of genes associated with memory decline in both groups, olfactory receptors were shown to be prominent, with 11 subtypes identified (Tables S6, S7 in supporting information). Finally, several genes identified as associated with R‐OFR in FAD make part of protein functional networks (Figure S7 in supporting information). We also identified that some genes harbored a high number of SNPs associated with specific variables such as R‐OFR or the memory domain in FAD (Figure 3A and Table 3 ). Of these, DDR1 for R‐OFR and C6orf10 for the memory domain showed a remarkably high number of SNPs, with 122 and 114, respectively. Other genes associated with R‐OFR and the memory domain decline were located in the 21.34p region of chromosome 6, and on a cluster of olfactory receptors in the 15.4p region in chromosome 11 (Figure 3B and Table S7 ). Interestingly, 508 genetic variants associated with RCD in the FAD group were located in in chromosome 6, while 126 genetic variants closely linked to olfactory receptors were identified in chromosome 11 (Table 3 and Table S7 ). We assessed those genes with both the strongest association on our SNP‐based analyses and the highest number of SNPs according to VEGAS2 (Figure 3C ). Interestingly, once the effect of abnormal variants on R‐OFR longitudinal performance was assessed, their contribution to R‐OFR decline became clear. In the case of the olfactory receptor OR51B6 , a single patient could contain as many as six variants in this gene, and the accumulation of variants shows an effect on R‐OFR decline. In addition, several patients presented variants in more than one gene, possibly generating a cumulative effect (Figure 3C ). The accumulation of &gt; 80 variants in these genes has a direct impact on R‐OFR performance in normal to MCI FAD patients. We repeated the association analysis in FAD patients taking into consideration cognitive decline evaluated only when patients were assessed normal or with MCI. The number of cognitive variables showing association was reduced to eight, with memory tests and the memory domain still showing relevant associations (Figure S8 in supporting information). We identified that only 32 genetic variants were preserved from the previous association analysis but not necessarily to the same cognitive test, with R‐OFR and the memory domain showing more reproducibility between analyses (Table S8 in supporting information). Finally, eight new variants belonged to gene families already described in the previous analysis whether for FAD or SAD (Table S9 in supporting information). As a validation of our results, we consulted the GEO: GSE147528 dataset and its graphical representation ( https://kampmannlab.ucsf.edu/ad‐brain ) 47 for the cellular distribution and expression of the main genes identified in the VEGAS2 analyses ( VARS2 , DDR1 , CD248 , and OR51B6 ). VARS2 and DDR1 showed contrasting expression distribution, with predominant expression of VARS2 in neurons (Figure 4A‐C ), and predominant expression of DDR1 in glial cells. On the other hand, CD248 showed almost no expression in any cell type cluster and OR51B6 was not present in the dataset. Olfaction and olfactory receptors have been previously associated with AD progression. 48 However, there is not a clear role for olfactory receptors in the cellular biology of neurons not directly involved with olfactory function. 49 Given that among olfactory receptors OR51B6 was the one that presented with the highest number of variants affecting R‐OFR performance, and that we could not analyze its expression in the GEO: GSE147528 dataset, we used in situ hybridization to verify the expression of OR51B6 in brain tissue (Figure 4D‐E ). We detected scarce OR51B6 signal in neurons from all cortices, although it was more evident in large pyramidal neurons, particularly in the temporal cortex (Figure 4D‐E ). Thus, there is a potential functional role of VARS2 and OR51B6 genetic variants in neurons and of DDR1 in glial cells, that could have an impact in cognitive function and its decline in PSEN1 E280A FAD patients. Furthermore, the rate of verbal and visual memory decline was shown to be strongly influenced by genetic variants (Figure 2 ). Although the specific genes identified for PSEN1 FAD differ from those identified in SAD, common families of proteins were detected and might play a role in memory decline (Tables S4 and S 9 ). Interestingly, among memory tests in the CERAD battery, the immediate recall of the R‐OFR showed the clearest susceptibility for performance modification by genetic variants. The complexity of this test involving praxis and visual memory may result in higher sensitivity to cognitive decline, allowing the influence of genetic variability to become evident. 50 , 51 In addition, R‐OFR immediate recall has been used previously to assess visual memory in the PSEN1 E280A population. Carriers showed an association between visual memory performance and AD pathology in vivo, evaluated by positron emission tomography (PET). 52 We also found that in PSEN1 E280A FAD patients, a cluster of genes localized in chromosomes 6 and 11 showed highly statistically significant association to visual memory decline, as evaluated by R‐OFR, and to memory decline in general. The 6p21.33 region showed five genes (i.e., DDR1 , GTF2H4 , VARS2 , DPCR1 , and SFTA2 ) strongly associated to memory performance. Of these, DDR1 has been previously associated with AD playing a role in amyloid beta (Aβ) degradation; 53 GTF2H4 has been associated with multiple sclerosis, 54 lung cancer, 55 amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, 56 and schizophrenia; 57 mutations in VARS2 have been identified in patients suffering from mitochondrial encephalomyopathies; 58 and DPCR1 belongs to a subset of genes found to be associated with major neuropsychiatric disorders. 59 Conversely, genetic variants in SFTA2 have not been reported as associated with central nervous system disorders. On the other hand, the 6p21.32 region in chromosome 6 showed two relevant genes, HLA‐DQB1 and C6orf10 . Genetic variants in HLA‐DQB1 have been described as modulating susceptibility for AD, 60 and C6orf10 , also known as testis specific basic protein 1 (TSBP1), has been associated with schizophrenia. 61 Genes identified in chromosome 11 belong to an olfactory receptors (OR) gene cluster. Interestingly, several genetic variants harbored in other OR genes located in the same region were associated to visual memory and memory in general, for both FAD and SAD groups (Table S6 ). It has been suggested that OR could play a role in non‐chemosensory organs, including the brain, 49 , 62 and their gene expression has been detected as altered in several neurological and neurodegenerative diseases, 49 including AD. 48 In fact, olfactory impairment has been shown to be an early marker in AD and other neurodegenerative diseases, 55 , 56 and it could be associated with its pathogenesis. 63 A decrease in smell perception has been detected in people with AD in 90% of cases, 64 , 65 and several animal models have been developed to study the role of the olfactory epithelium in the pathways related to the pathogenesis of AD. 66 In addition, there seems to be a correlation between amyloid load and olfactory impairment, in contrast to the lack of correlation between amyloid load and cognitive impairment. 67 Copy number variants of olfactory genes associated with age of SAD onset 68 and olfactory tests are widely used as a diagnostic and predictive tool in AD clinical assessment. 69 , 70 From a pathological perspective, the olfactory bulb is involved in the early Braak stages 71 and correlates with cortical pathology. 72 , 73 Aβ plaques and tau neurofibrillary tangles have been identified in olfactory structures in the brain, and it should be noted that limbic structures, affected early in AD, provide direct afferent and efferent pathways to olfactory structures such as the olfactory bulb and piriform cortex. 74 It has also been postulated that the APP machinery in the olfactory epithelium differs from other parts of the brain. This is because accumulation of Aβ*56 has been found in the olfactory epithelium before transgenic mice show cognitive deficits. 75 Ectopic olfactory receptors, which are expressed in tissues other than the olfactory epithelium, have been frequently reported in recent years, and it is possible that they have functions other than those related to smell. 62 There are reports of detection of olfactory receptors in the frontal, entorhinal, and cerebellum cortex in post mortem brains of subjects with neurodegenerative diseases. 76 Recently, 35 individuals belonging to the PSEN1 E280A cohort, 16 of them non‐demented mutation carriers, were assessed for olfactory and cognitive tests contrasted to tau PET imaging. An association between worse olfactory performance and higher tau burden was identified in PSEN1 E280A carriers. 68 There is also evidence of inflammatory changes and altered myelination in the olfactory tract of post mortem tissue from PSEN1 E280A carriers. 75 The timeline of neurodegenerative events in the olfactory bulb of this population still needs to be determined. Once it is clarified, the evaluation of the association between smell perception dysfunction and the genetic variants identified in this study will be possible. Expression of OR in non‐sensory neurons has been previously identified in substantia nigra and specifically OR2H2 , OR2A4 , and OR6K3 have been detected in other cortical and subcortical structures. 49 In post mortem transcriptome analysis of the olfactory bulb in humans with varying degrees of AD pathology, negatively regulated OR2T2 and OR5M1 olfactory receptors were observed in intermediate stages, and positively regulated OR2T8 and OR6J1 genes in advanced stages, indicating different receptor regulation in the olfactory bulb for AD. 65 , 66 Studies in human carriers of APOE ε4 have shown early olfactory dysfunction, impaired smell identification, and altered olfactory‐related evoked potentials. 66 The possible role of an olfactory receptor as a proinflammatory chemosensor is raised by identifying Olfr110 differentially regulated according to APOE alleles during induced microglia activation. 65 , 77 Here we showed that OR51B6 , one of the genes with the strongest association between genetic variants and rate of memory decline, is expressed in low amounts in cortical neurons. However, the functional role OR516B plays in these neurons and their possible impact in memory processes remains to be explored. Finally, acceleration of memory decline associated with a specific genetic profile was shown to be higher in early stages of dementia. It is possible that in later stages of AD the memory tests in use for dementia assessment are no longer sensitive enough to detect the effect of the genetic variants identified in this study. Recently, in a larger sample of PSEN1 E280A carriers, an association between APOE ε4 allele and faster cognitive decline was identified. 12 Sample size could be considered a limitation of the present study. For that finding, we did not identify an association between slope of cognitive decline and APOE alleles (Table S10 in supporting information). Finding a larger sample of FAD patients with the genetic homogeneity and structured follow‐up of those of the PSEN1 E280A population represents a challenge. We believe that the methodological and analytic approaches of this study compensate for sample size up to a point and that they support the conclusions. Other limitations of this study include the heterogeneity of pharmacological intervention in both populations during their follow‐up, and its possible impact in RCD. Previously we have identified that FAD cases receive more often antiepileptic medication than SAD patients. 20 Because a positive effect on cognitive decline of these drugs has been reported for AD, 78 and FAD cases showed a more severe cognitive decline than SAD patients, we consider that a drug‐related effect in our findings is unlikely. Even though progressive and severe memory impairment is a main feature in AD, RCD varies between patients, with some patients showing accelerated decline and reaching dependency earlier in their disease. 79 Recently we have identified two PSEN1 E280A carriers presenting with dementia onset &gt; 25 years later than the average for PSEN1 E280A carriers, together with distinct neuropathological profile. Notably, disease protection in each case was associated with a different mutation in another gene. 25 , 26 This degree of protection is an indicator of the impact of genetic variants in the heterogeneity of disease presentation in this population, and of its suitability as a disease model for discovering relevant phenotype modifiers. Thus, the identification of genetic markers in accelerated memory decline in both FAD and SAD not only could bring us a clue for other pathological mechanisms in AD but also become targets of future multi‐stage therapies for addressing cognitive decline in this disorder.
