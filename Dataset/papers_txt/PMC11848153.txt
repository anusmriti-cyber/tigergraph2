Abstract More than 2 million older Americans from underrepresented racial and ethnic minority groups (URGs) have early‐stage Alzheimer's disease and related dementias (ADRD). There are very few scalable recruitment strategies, particularly for Black older adults, to accelerate participation in ADRD research. The Indiana Alzheimer's Disease Research Center (IADRC) and its Community Advisory Board developed and implemented the innovative RAAISE‐D Framework. This Framework informed the creation of community‐first recruitment strategies designed to accelerate participation of Black older adults in ADRD research. Preliminary outcomes from its implementation included the doubling of Black adult enrollment (46, 13.4% to 101, 26.9%) from April 2020 to April 2024. Black adults were more likely to have normal cognition, be female, and ≤ 12 years of education than non‐Hispanic White adults. The RAAISE‐D Framework identified key concepts for URG focused recruitment strategies which successfully accelerated enrollment of Black adults in ADRD research and could be generalized to other URGs. Highlights RAAISE‐D Framework provides adaptable URG recruitment strategies. IADRC CAB‐researcher partnership was the foundation of community‐first methodology. potentially scalable recruitment strategies to accelerate URG participation in ADRD research Over 2 million older Americans from underrepresented racial and ethnic minority groups (URGs) have early‐stage Alzheimer's disease and related dementias (ADRD). 1 Despite the widespread recognition that more inclusive early‐stage ADRD research could reduce this public health burden, URG research participation remains alarmingly low, and is particularly evidenced by the less than 5% enrollment rate of Black older adults in recent ADRD clinical drug trials. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 Previous literature has primarily offered high‐level recommendations aimed at mitigating this disproportionately low URG representation, such as removing restrictive study criteria, addressing concerns surrounding trust in researchers and healthcare professionals, and enhancing communication with diverse communities to underscore the significance of research participation. 6 , 7 , 8 Significant knowledge gaps persist within the field of recruitment science, particularly regarding the development of data‐driven, ``real‐world’’ scalable recruitment strategies, including ADRD neuroimaging studies and clinical trials. 9 , 10 Moreover, there is a pressing need to understand how to pragmatically implement such strategies and effectively track the progress of these recruitment strategies. 11 , 12 1.2 Challenges to developing scalable, “real‐world” recruitment strategies In 2010, the first conceptual models of recruitment science in ADRD research emphasized the importance of diversity, community engagement, and an inclusive approach to research. 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 Over the past decade, ADRD research has expanded to include multisite neuroimaging studies and clinical trials requiring completion of biomarker procedures as part of a precision medicine approach. 18 , 19 While the research collection of biomarker data has been instrumental to the first United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved disease modifying anti‐amyloid agents, the introduction of these procedures present significant research participant burden. 20 , 21 , 22 It also can be a barrier to making studies more inclusive and exacerbate longstanding research disparities in ADRD. 23 Deeply understanding the challenges to inclusive research participation is critical to addressing this need to create scalable, ``real‐world’’ strategies specifically to accelerate enrollment of URG participants in multisite studies requiring neuroimaging and biomarker procedures. To address these challenges of developing scalable, ``real‐world’’ recruitment strategies, a team of our OREC (Outreach, Recruitment, and Engagement Core) faculty and the Community Advisory Board (CAB) leadership reviewed the literature and had various brainstorming sessions with our IADRC leadership and local URG community leaders. We observed certain recurring themes within the IADRC and across the ADRC network. These included: (1) external, unpredictable factors affecting outreach and recruitment (e.g., coronavirus disease 2019 [COVID‐19] pandemic, timing of FDA approval for AD modifying drugs, changes in relationships with community partners); (2) a lack of processes to analyze and address inevitable failures from the trial‐and‐error process of recruitment; (3) variability in local resources for outreach and recruitment; and (4) time constraints to achieve specified goals. We identified three challenges to developing scalable ``real‐world’’ recruitment strategies: (1) strategic problem‐solving processes to identify strategies which meet proposed targets, (2) consensus about the targets and interim indicators of these targets, and (3) tracking of progress toward these targets (Table 1 ). As the OREC and CAB reflected on strategic problem‐solving processes, we recognized that the traditional scientific approach is to develop a high impact, abstract vision in an academic setting. To fulfill this vision, researchers often develop a priori recruitment strategies with highly innovative ideas which will solve a problem that has not been answered before—in this case, URG recruitment into ADRD research, which included neuroimaging studies. The hypotheses underlying these strategies are designed to be tested under ``highly controlled conditions.’’ Another major challenge to developing ``real‐world’’ strategies is the measurement of targets and indicators, including use of metrics which are setting or study specific and publication of targets (e.g., enrolled individuals who complete study procedures) and leading indicators (novel methodologies to increase research referrals) discretely rather than concurrently. The third major challenge is methodology for tracking progress toward the proposed targets, often focusing on retrospectively analyzing the failures to meet leading and lagging indicators and lacks interim projections for leading and lagging indicators. This article aims to describe the conceptualization of an innovative Framework, designed to increase URG participation in ADRD research. We will also report on preliminary outcomes following its implementation, including an acceleration of Black older adult enrollment in the IADRC cohort (from 46/343, 13.4% to 101/376, 26.9%) in a 4‐year period (2020–2024). We will also discuss lessons learned and the next steps for creating potentially scalable recruitment strategies that can be disseminated across multisite studies. 2 METHODS 2.1 Development of a conceptual Framework for “real‐world” recruitment strategies To accelerate URG recruitment into ADRD research, we proposed our novel RAAISE‐D Framework ( R eal‐World A gile, A daptive, I nnovative Learning S ystems to E ngage D iverse Communities). There are three major goals of this Framework. First, how do we create a Framework to solve a problem that has not yet been solved before? Second, how do we create a Framework to efficiently generate readily testable solutions in a dynamic environment which is often highly unpredictable because of external factors (e.g., COVID‐19 pandemic, FDA approval of AD modifying drugs) and internal factors (e.g., institutional IRB changes)? Third, once we identify a solution, how do we methodically adapt it in response to ongoing changes and in other settings which have their own specific needs? During our initial discussions, our CAB members made several key insights. First, they reiterated that, to successfully increase URG representation in the IADRC cohort, earning the trust of the community was critical. However, despite widespread recognition of the importance of trust to increase research enrollment, there are very few, if any, rigorously conducted trials which have evidence‐based approaches to build trust in research naïve URG participants and rapidly accelerate enrollment for multisite studies. 9 , 24 , 25 Furthermore, trust varies over time and is affected by personal context. How would our team know if they had effectively built trust in the community, if we cannot easily and quickly measure in real‐time this key metric in real‐world settings? Another key insight was that given the high complexity of the factors identified in the literature and their own diverse viewpoints about ADRD research based on their own personal experiences, the team recognized that a single recruitment strategy would not be effective at addressing this question. Instead, they proposed that our team collaboratively develop serial strategies to build trust within the community and empirically determine whether these strategies resulted in meaningful change in interim indicators and outcomes (in this case, increased IADRC enrollment). Furthermore, these strategies, even successful ones, need to be regularly adapted to respond to the dynamic environment. How would our team ensure constant bidirectional communication between academia and the community? Finally, there was the realization that, given the years of effort with minimal impact on this key project, failures would inevitably be part of the process of finding a pathway to success. How would our team learn from these failures to reach target enrollment goals? 2.2 Implementation science processes underlying the RAAISE‐D Framework To address these three goals and incorporate these critical insights made by our CAB, we created the ``RAAISE‐D Framework.’’ This Framework merges three implementation science processes and models specifically selected to address our goals: the Learning Health System, Agile Implementation Process, and the Model for Adaptation Design and Impact (MADI). The Learning Health System was first described in 2007 by the United States Institute of Medicine (now the National Academy of Medicine), 26 which later defined it as a system in which ``science, informatics, incentives, and culture are aligned for continuous improvement and innovation, with best practices seamlessly embedded in the delivery process, [where] patients and families [are] active participants in all elements, and new knowledge [is] captured as an integral by‐product of the delivery experience’’. 27 The focus is on collecting data to generate knowledge and applying it to improve practice. To successfully create a learning health system, a technical expert panel first identified eight competency domains: (1) engagement, leadership, and research management; (2) ethics of research and implementation in health systems; (3) health and healthcare equity and justice; (4) improvement and implementation science; (5) informatics; (6) research methods; (7) research questions and standards of scientific evidence; and (8) systems science. Table S1 outlines the Learning Health system competency domains, the domain definitions, and competencies. 28 , 29 The Agile Implementation process seeks to broadly explain people's decision‐making processes and behavior through system 1 (intuitive, instantaneous decision‐making) and system 2 (logical, slower decision‐making). In this model, each individual operates within a complex adaptive system (i.e., a system in which semi‐autonomous agents interact). Therefore, the behavior of an individual can affect the entire system, directly or indirectly. The Agile Implementation process seeks to change outcomes through ``nudges’’ created by one of these semi‐autonomous agents. 30 , 31 , 32 For example, nudges created by the CAB to participate in research are likely to lead the community to intuitively gravitate toward research participation because members of the community have designed the process, thus their fellow community members will ``feel’’ that it is right to say ``yes’’ to participation (system 1). As we reflected on how to use the Agile Implementation process, we decided to utilize Agile Nudge Innovation because there are no pre‐existing evidence‐based, minimally viable nudges. 33 Figure S1 describes in detail the eight steps in the Agile Implementation process (Agile Nudge Innovation) and how these nudges are planned (confirm the demand for a nudge; study the behavior deeply; scan for potential nudges; plan termination for new nudge) and executed (ideate and select new nudges; run nudge sprints; validate nudges; and package launch for nudge). 34 The MADI complements the Agile Implementation Process and guides an iterative adaptation process to modify the proposed nudges prospectively or retrospectively. 35 Figure S2 outlines the three main domains of MADI: domain 1: adaptation characteristics, which provides consistency in reporting of adaptation to promote comparison of findings across studies/sites; domain 2: possible mediating or moderating factors, which provides criteria for prospectively making adaptations or retrospectively explaining why and how outcomes are achieved; and domain 3: outcomes, which examines potential impact on intervention and implementation outcomes to make more informed adaptation decisions (prospective application) or evaluations (retrospective application). 36 , 37 , 38 2.3 Components of the RAAISE‐D Framework The RAAISE‐D Framework (Figure 1A ) consists of the following components: (1) R eal‐World Data Core, (2) Community First A gile Implementation, (3) Iterative A daptation, and (4) Community First I nnovative Learning S ystem, which have been modified to e ngage d iverse communities. R eal‐World Data Core (Figure 1B ): R eal‐World Data Driven Strategies are created through a cycle of diverse community participation in research, which generates real‐world data, and then leads to inclusive scientific knowledge. Sharing this scientific knowledge with diverse communities re‐engages them in the research process and further emphasizes the importance of their involvement in research. The Community First nudges, adaptations to diverse community needs, and innovative ideas that drive this cycle are critical to accelerating URG participation in ADRD research. Community First A gile Implementation (Figure 1C ): To successfully initiate this cycle of diverse community participation, there is a need to successfully create nudges that accelerate URG recruitment in ADRD research. One key adaptation is that these must be Community First nudges, that is, nudges designed by the CAB and then validated by the URG communities. The modified steps are: (1) Identify the community's demand for a Community First nudge to accelerate URG participation in ADRD research; (2) Study the community's behavior deeply; (3) Scan for potential Community First nudges; (4) Plan termination for new Community First nudge; (5) Ideate and select new Community First nudges; (6) Run Community First nudge sprints; (7) Validate Community First nudges; and (8) Launch Community First nudges more widely. Iterative A daptation of Community First Nudges (Figure 1D ): As the Community First nudges are launched more widely and external factors arise, that either facilitate (e.g., FDA approval of novel AD modifying drugs), impede (COVID‐19 pandemic), or have unknown impact (e.g., new National Institute on Aging [NIA] guidance as part of a request for funding announcement), adaptations to these Community First Nudges will be needed. Real‐time observations of community responses are used to develop new recruitment strategies. Community First I nnovative Learning S ystem (Figure 1E ): Innovation is essential to continuously designing new Community First nudges, successfully adapting these nudges in response to external factors, and, most importantly, building and strengthening a learning system with continuous information exchange between the URG community and academia. Similar to the identified competency domains for the Learning Health System, we identified the following modified domains needed for an Innovative Learning System: (1) community engagement, community and academic leadership, and community‐engaged research management; (2) ethics of community‐engaged research and implementation in health systems; (3) community health and healthcare equity; (4) improvement and implementation science; (5) informatics; (6) community‐engaged research methods; (7) community‐engaged research questions and standards of scientific evidence; and (8) systems science which incorporates community input. OREC engages in community outreach, including events with URG focused organizations, faith‐based organizations, and health fairs. ORE Core identifies prospective IADRC participants through personal referrals from faculty, staff, CAB, and other research participants; at community outreach events; online; and referrals from healthcare professionals. ORE Core then screens participants to determine eligibility based on inclusion/exclusion criteria (Table S2 ). Of note, because the IADRC is primarily focused on neuroimaging research, site specific inclusion criteria require participants to be interested and eligible to do a 3.0T magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and positron emission tomography (PET) scans at the time of their screening. At enrollment, IADRC participants undergo informed consent or if unable to consent, will have a legally authorized representative consent on their behalf. Participants then proceed to their baseline visit with Clinical Core, which includes completion of the NACC UDS 3.0. The NACC UDS 3.0 contains detailed questionnaires about demographics, family history, medical, and psychiatric disorders, and a neuropsychological battery. Informants also complete questionnaires about participants’ cognition, functioning and neuropsychiatric symptoms. Upon completion of this visit, participants go on to complete a brain MRI and amyloid and tau PET scans. 2.5 Building the infrastructure for real‐world data core and community first innovative learning system A major barrier to accelerating URG recruitment into the IADRC was when all participants were required to be both eligible for and interested in completing neuroimaging procedures (MRI and PET scans) to enroll in the IADRC. 39 This inclusion criterion was added because the National Institutes of Health (NIH) grants funding the IADRC primarily focused on neuroimaging. Therefore, the OREC and CAB leadership recognized the need to develop and test recruitment strategies tailored to prospective URG participants. Black adults were the primary focus for the IADRC because they are the most prevalent ethnoracial minority in Indiana. 40 The IADRC OREC and CAB leadership conducted a series of brainstorming sessions to discuss ways to effectively engage Black communities in order to boost outreach, recruitment, and ultimately, enrollment into the IADRC. These leaders first identified promising recruitment strategies to increase Black participation. Content expertise included CAB leadership, OREC, Administrative Core, Data Core, and Neuroimaging Core. Previous literature was not definitive on which recruitment strategy would be the most successful: (1) directly approaching research naïve Black individuals about participating in procedure intensive studies requiring neuroimaging or (2) enrolling Black individuals in research with minimal procedures and then reapproaching them about joining a study requiring additional procedures. 41 , 42 About 1 month into this discussion, the COVID‐19 pandemic began, pausing enrollment in neuroimaging research. This pause presented a unique opportunity for this team to consider novel ways for increasing Black participation in ADRD research, including neuroimaging studies. They also recognized that to design and evaluate successful recruitment strategies in the post‐pandemic era, the infrastructure for the Real‐World Data Core and the Community First Innovative Learning System needed to be built. This infrastructure would then guide and measure real‐world data‐driven recruitment efforts. In 2020, IADRC obtained a NIA‐funded P30 supplement (AD‐REACH) to design real‐world data‐driven recruitment strategies which would accelerate Black participation in the IADRC neuroimaging studies. As the first step, the IADRC engaged the CAB more broadly to develop a survey to better understand the attitudes about brain health, Alzheimer's disease, and ADRD research, including neuroimaging studies, in the Black communities in the Indianapolis metropolitan area. These findings have been described elsewhere. 43 In addition to informing the science of recruitment for URG participation in research, the AD‐REACH supplement was also critical to the infrastructure expansion of the IADRC and initial implementation of the RAAISE‐D Framework: (1) it initiated the cycle of the Real‐World Data Core; (2) it laid the foundation for the Community First Innovative Learning System; and (3) it created a culture of shared values and expertise across diverse communities (CAB) and academia (IADRC OREC). The ultimate result was that the CAB and IADRC OREC being strongly aligned about their vision for diverse research participation, goals, recruitment strategies, and content expertise who recognized the need for deep and extensive investment to achieve a target goal as part of a larger, sustainable pattern of growth. Together, they have developed and tested a wide range of Community First Nudges. John Wiley &amp; Sons, Ltd. 2.6 Data infrastructure for collection and visualization of URG recruitment processes The next step to the implementation of the RAAISE‐D Framework was to build the data infrastructure for collecting and visualizing the outreach and recruitment processes to capture the acceleration of URG enrollment. The IADRC OREC and CAB leadership co‐led the operationalization of recruitment processes in a series of meetings with Clinical Core and Data Core staff, which led to the creation of the database infrastructure (Figure S5 ) and dashboards (Figure S3B ) to capture overall trends for strategy development and individual level data for practical troubleshooting. While the primary goal was to design databases and dashboards which focus on accelerating enrollment of Black older adults, the predominant racial minority residing in the Indianapolis metropolitan area, the leadership anticipated that we would be adapting this infrastructure and dashboards for other URGs and other affiliated studies (e.g., DiverseVCID, CLARiTI). Therefore, these conversations reflected on how to build the infrastructure so they could be generalized for other URGs and other diversity focused ADRD studies. Another key insight was that, although study enrollment is a static, lagging indicator, the leading indicators (referrals, screened participants, scheduled participants) reflect a dynamic, multistep process. Thus, the dashboards needed to visualize the leading indicators in real‐time and reflect the underlying processes (outreach and engagement, referral, screening, scheduling, and enrollment in Clinical Core) which connect the leading and lagging indicators (Figure S3B ). Another key insight was how to collect data which connect a broad strategy (e.g., community outreach and engagement, healthcare professional referral) to the first measurable datapoint at individual level (e.g., research referral) with the connecting question of how that individual was referred. After the OREC obtained feedback from all the other cores and CAB about mock dashboards, the Data Core finalized these dashboards using Tableau, a tool that visualizes key information from our REDCap databases, which tracked key leading and lagging indicators (Figure S3C ). 47 After building the dashboards, we then designed weekly performance reports which tracked the leading and lagging indicators. We also built REDCap notifications for key performance metrics (e.g., notifications to remind staff to attempt another contact if participants did not respond to an earlier contact). Our standardized operating procedures described how the data were collected and how we used these operational reports to identify challenges and solutions at each step of the recruitment process to improve recruitment science (Figure S3D ). 3 RESULTS 3.1 Impact of RAAISE‐D Framework and demographics Prior to the implementation of the RAAISE‐D Framework, 46 Black adults enrolled in the IADRC from 2009 to 2020. We implemented the RAAISE‐D Framework in April 2020, when OREC set up regular meetings with the CAB leadership to co‐create innovative ideas (``Community First Nudges’’) to increase research participation of Black communities in central Indiana. We received 1137 referrals and enrolled 64 Black adults from 2020 to 2024, which included a time period during the pandemic when in‐person research procedures were paused (Figure S6 ). Our systematic process allowed us to identify barriers to accelerating URG enrollment at the pre‐screening and screening stages. This process consisted of reviewing the weekly tracking reports for outreach attendance, referrals, recruitment, and enrollment and discussing these findings from the real‐time dashboards with the CAB. We also conducted post‐event feedback sessions with CAB members to obtain real‐time feedback about Community First Nudges to refine them for the next community event. Demographics (i.e., URG status) were confirmed during the screening stage. Only 36% (407/1137) of all research referrals were screened, in which 41% (167/407) of screened referrals identified as Black. Of those who were screened, only 35% (142/407) were eligible for enrollment, resulting in about 12% of referrals being enrolled. The enrollment rate for those who completed screening and identified as Black (38.3% [64/167]) was slightly higher than those who identified as non‐Hispanic White (32.9% [75/228]). Community‐based referrals were more likely to be uncontactable (213, 18.7%) compared to electronic (127, 11.2%) and healthcare professional referrals (32, 2.8%) (Table S7 ). Although we only had limited ethnoracial data at the pre‐screening stage, we were concerned that our low success of contact rates for URG participants, mostly because these events were based in URG communities. As part of our systematic process, we shared with our CAB leadership the real‐time visualization from our dashboards and discussed our concerns about the lower rates of contact with community‐based referrals. Our CAB leadership gave feedback that community members were likely not responding because our calls were being blocked or were coming from unrecognized numbers since community members had not had previous contact with the IADRC recruiter. Based on this CAB feedback about the dashboard tracking, OREC decided to incorporate Mosio texting into our recruitment strategy so prospective participants would be able to recognize the IADRC phone number. We also examined reasons for exclusion at screening and found the top two reasons were fairly similar for non‐Hispanic White and Black adults (other, mainly due to age [White: 74, 58.3%; Black: 31, 45.6%]; medical, neurologic, or psychiatric diagnoses (White: 30, 23.6%; Black: 27, 39.7%) (Table S8 ). 4 DISCUSSION Despite widespread recognition of the public health importance of diverse participation in ADRD biomarker research, there are very few, if any, evidence‐based recruitment strategies specifically designed to increase URG participation in ADRD research. 2 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 The COVID‐19 pandemic (2020–2023) was a period of unprecedented national challenges for recruitment and enrollment in ADRD research studies. The pandemic also underscored the health disparities disproportionately affecting diverse communities and the need to develop innovative recruitment strategies to successfully engage them in ADRD research. 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 On the other hand, the unintended pause created by the COVID‐19 pandemic provided a unique space for the IADRC OREC and CAB to create a new conceptual Framework which would inform a potentially generalizable approach to accelerate URG participation in ADRD research. The innovative RAAISE‐D Framework sought to identify promising multipronged strategies to accelerate Black enrollment in ADRD research, including neuroimaging studies. This Framework demonstrated the value of a Community First approach to enrolling URG participants in the post‐pandemic era. 4.1 Successes of the implementation of RAAISE‐D Framework Through our monthly meetings with the IADRC OREC and CAB and applying our innovative Framework, we were able to accelerate research participation of Black older adults, at the end of the COVID‐19 pandemic and post‐pandemic era. Our primary insight is that study recruitment is complex and unpredictable. In response to this insight, our team decided to expand our traditional scientific approach of ``siloed’’ specialization and hypothesis‐driven testing and created the RAAISE‐D Framework to incorporate agile implementation, systematic adaptation, and innovative lifelong learning. While the enrolled Black older adults skewed toward females and those with normal cognition, the Framework has already been designed to adapt our strategies to anticipate referral biases during its second cycle of implementation. The generalizability of this Framework is also another key question. The RAAISE‐D Framework is not specific to geographic or an URG. Future studies will need to test whether implementation of this Framework can also be used to rapidly accelerate enrollment at other geographic sites, other URGs, and a range of brain disorders beyond ADRD.
