Asymptomatic Alzheimer’s Disease (AsymAD) is a preclinical stage of Alzheimer’s Disease (AD) identified by amyloid plaques and neurofibrillary tangles in cognitively normal individuals and offers essential understanding for early diagnosis and treatment of AD. To uncover molecular insights into AsymAD, RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) datasets from two different consortia, ROSMAP (Religious Orders Study and Memory and Aging Project) and MSBB (Mount Sinai Brain Bank), were investigated. The individuals in the datasets were grouped into AD and AsymAD based on clinical and neuropathological criteria. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs), differentially expressed transcripts (DETs), and differentially used transcripts (DUTs) were identified between AD and AsymAD samples. The results were interpreted through functional enrichment analysis and compared with the predefined lists of AD-related and learning-memory-cognition-related genes, and genes from an independent mouse dataset. The genes from the list of DEGs, DETs and DUTs were mapped onto a human protein-protein interaction network, revealing subnetworks associated with AsymAD. This led to the discovery of biomarker candidate genes: NRXN3 , DGKB , ADAMTS2 , GNG4 , ENPP5 , PCOLCE , COL25A1 , COL26A1 , MRPL1 , and MRPL30 . This study introduces an innovative approach by including DETs and DUTs in the analyses, beyond the standard focus on DEGs, pointing out comprehensive insights into the molecular mechanisms of AsymAD. In addition, combining the results of the subnetwork analysis from DEGs, DETs, and DUTs provided a new perspective to AsymAD and resulted in the discovery of further important genes, which can pave the way for early detection and intervention of AD. These aggregates, which are formed precisely in the cognitive parts of the brain, trigger a cascade of problems at the molecular and cellular levels, subsequently causing cognitive decline in the brain over time, and eventual death (Busche and Hyman 2020 ). Although some treatments alleviate symptoms and improve the quality of life, it is well known that there is no cure once the disease starts (Knopman et al. 2021 ). Therefore, management of the disease and finding permanent solutions are of great importance. Asymptomatic Alzheimer’s Disease (AsymAD) is a disorder in which there is neuropathology compatible with AD, yet there is no obvious cognitive decline before death (Monsell et al. 2014 ). Accordingly, research on AsymAD is crucial and promising for the early detection of patients who are at risk for AD and early intervention in the disease process (Crous-Bou et al. 2017 ; Driscoll and Troncoso 2011 ). AsymAD, which is also known as the preclinical stage of AD, sheds light on the multifaceted nature of AD progression. Conventional approaches are insufficient to address heterogeneity and the preclinical stages of AD. Systems biology approaches, such as transcriptomics, proteomics, and lipidomics, provide a more comprehensive perspective on the molecular mechanisms underlying complex diseases (Marques-Coelho et al. 2021 ). Previous omics-based AsymAD studies identified protein modules linked to AD progression, which suggested changes in synaptic function, inflammation, and RNA metabolism (Seyfried et al. 2017 ), showed a multi-omics signature of AsymAD by revealing early molecular changes such as alterations in inflammation-related gene expression and lipid metabolism (Xicota et al. 2019 ), and identified the role of RNA-related mechanisms in AD progression (Swarup et al. 2020 ). Moreover, a study by Patel et al. ( 2019 ) highlighted the importance of transcriptomic studies on AsymAD by suggesting potential dementia resistance pathways through the analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and network analysis, including the upregulation of synaptic plasticity genes and downregulation of inflammatory genes (Patel et al. 2019 ). However, these studies have not attempted to extract information hidden in RNA-seq datasets beyond differential gene expression, and they rarely integrate transcriptomic data with network validation or explore gene interactions across different models or datasets, which limits the understanding of the functional relevance of AsymAD. Moreover, they did not provide a detailed discussion on prominent pathways in specifically comparing AD and AsymAD. In transcriptomics, the most widely used method is the identification of DEGs. However, transcriptomics can also detect changes in the levels of transcript isoforms through differentially expressed transcript (DET) and differentially used transcript (DUT) analyses, shedding light on molecular changes such as alternative splicing events. Several studies have highlighted the importance of these methods over conventional DEG analysis. For instance, Marques-Coelho et al. ( 2021 ) complemented the DEG analysis with the DUT analysis to examine changes at the gene and transcript levels in AD. The DEG analysis revealed significant pathways like synaptic function, neuroinflammation, and energy metabolism, while the DUT analysis revealed transcript-specific changes that may be overlooked at the gene level, such as alternative splicing (Marques-Coelho et al. 2021 ). It is critical to distinguish isoforms because they might encode proteins with distinct functions in subcellular localisations and may also generate no protein product (Dick et al. 2020 ). Furthermore, Wu et al. ( 2023 ) investigated transcriptional changes associated with the progression of AD through DET and DUT analyses. According to the findings, isoform changes and alternative splicing events played an important role in the molecular mechanisms of AD and AsymAD. Specifically, in AsymAD, DETs were enriched in metabolic and memory-related pathways, while DUTs were enriched in dendrite development and amyloid-beta regulation (Wu et al. 2023 ). These studies showed that incorporating DET and DUT methods in addition to the commonly used DEG method offers a more comprehensive approach to understanding the molecular mechanisms of AD. Although these studies emphasise the importance and usage of these three methods, they did not analyse DEGs, DETs, and DUTs in a combined manner in AD or AsymAD to better visualise gene and transcript-level changes. Exclusively, a study on major depressive disorder is the only one that applies these three methods (DEG, DET, and DUT) together (de Souza et al. 2024 ). Here, we present a detailed molecular characterisation of AsymAD, filling a large gap in the literature, through two distinct datasets by applying a multi-level transcriptomic methodology that combines the findings of DEGs, DETs, and DUTs on AD vs. AsymAD comparison. We identify novel candidate biomarker genes and pathways specific to AsymAD, which could shed significant light on understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying AsymAD and eventually contribute to the early diagnosis of AD. The novelty of this study is the consideration of transcript-level results, DETs and DUTs, in addition to DEGs, with a network-based approach, while the standard analysis in the literature is mostly DEG-based and does not integrate DEG, DET, and DUT analyses altogether. At a time when AD takes a heavy toll and effective treatments are scarce, studying AsymAD is crucial for early detection and prevention of AD progression. Methods RNA-seq Datasets and Data Pre-processing The RNA-seq datasets used in this study were the Dorsolateral Prefrontal Cortex region of the ROSMAP (Bennett et al. 2018 ) and the Frontal Pole (FP) region of the MSBB (Wang et al., 2018 ) datasets, which are among the most commonly used RNA-seq datasets in AD. The FP region of MSBB was specifically chosen because it is located in a brain region where it is designated as the “cognition” region according to Brodmann’s area (BA) classification (Zilles and Amunts 2010 ). Raw fastq reads were previously downloaded from the Synapse platform (synapse.org) with the accession IDs syn17024112 for ROSMAP and syn7416949 and syn8612191 for MSBB, and quality-controlled and trimmed in our research group (Düz et al. 2024 ; Lüleci et al. 2024 ). The pre-processed fastq files were then pseudo-aligned to the GRCh38 human reference transcriptome obtained from GENCODE (gencodegenes.org) with the Kallisto (version 0.46.1) package (Bray et al. 2016 ). This process involved indexing the reference transcriptome and estimating the abundance of the transcripts in the datasets using pseudo-alignment. Transcript-level expression quantifications were imported for all samples using the tximport package (version 1.30.0) (Soneson et al. 2015 ). In addition to extracting transcript-level data, the same function was used to generate gene-level count estimates. Grouping Datasets and Identifying the AsymAD Group The AD and AsymAD groups were created based on the clinical and pathological classifications in both datasets. For the ROSMAP dataset, the AsymAD group was identified by selecting individuals with the Consortium to Establish a Registry for Alzheimer’s Disease (CERAD) pathological scores 1 (Definite AD) and 2 (Probable AD) and the Cognitive Diagnosis (COGDX) clinical score 0 falling into the No Cognitive Impairment (NCI) category (Supplementary Table 1 ). Similarly, for the MSBB dataset, the AsymAD individuals were identified as those with 1 or 2 CERAD pathological scores and 0 (NCI) Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) score (Supplementary Table 2 ). The mean age at death of AsymAD individuals was 85.9 years in the ROSMAP dataset and 81.6 years in the MSBB dataset. The mean age at death of AD individuals was 87.9 years in ROSMAP and 83.1 years in MSBB. AD and control samples were defined using cohort-specific clinical and pathological criteria. In ROSMAP, individuals classified as AD met the criteria of COGDX = 4 or 5 with CERAD scores of 1 or 2. Controls had COGDX scores of 1–3 and CERAD scores of 3 or 4. In MSBB, AD samples were defined by a Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) score ≥ 1 and a CERAD score of 1 or 2. Controls had CDR ≤ 0.5, with CERAD scores of 3 or 4. A summary of group definitions and the number of samples across two cohorts is provided in Supplementary Table 3 . Normalisation and Covariate Adjustment The CPM filtration with the cpm function from the edgeR (version 3.43.8) (Robinson et al. 2009 ) package was used to filter out low counts and balance the library sizes. Genes with CPM values higher than 0.1 for the n number of samples, where n is the number of samples in the smaller group between the two groups to be compared, were kept in the dataset. This led to 22,303 genes and 104,980 transcripts for the ROSMAP data and 30,591 genes and 164,566 transcripts for the MSBB data. The filtered count data were normalised with the normalisation step of the DESeq2 (version 1.42.1) package (Love et al. 2014 ). Next, PCA was performed to check for any outliers in the datasets. Two outliers (Sample ID: 367_120502 and 500_120515) from the AD group were removed in the ROSMAP samples, while no outlier was identified in the MSBB samples. To reduce the effects of covariates on the data, the covariate adjustment method previously used by Lüleci et al. ( 2024 ) was adopted (Lüleci et al. 2024 ). In this step, gender, age, and post-mortem interval (PMI) values from the metadata were used as covariates in a linear model using the lm function in R to identify and remove the effects of these covariates on each DESeq2-normalised gene expression value. DEG, DET, and DUT Analyses The normalised and covariate-adjusted data were used for the DEG and DET analyses with the limma package in R (version 3.57.7) (Ritchie et al. 2015 ) to determine differentially expressed genes and transcripts for the comparison of the two groups. A different method was followed for the DUT analysis using the DRIMSeq package in R (version 1.30.0) (Nowicka and Robinson 2016 ). Additionally, the covariate adjustment method used for the DEG and DET analyses was implemented in the DUT analysis by the model.matrix function of the DRIMSeq package. The p -value cut-off was determined as 0.001 for the ROSMAP dataset and 0.01 for the MSBB dataset for all three analyses. Due to the differences in sample size and statistical power between the two cohorts, dataset-specific unadjusted p -value thresholds were applied to balance sensitivity and specificity for the downstream integrative analyses. Under the final thresholds, the proportion of significant features remained below 5% across all analyses in both ROSMAP and MSBB cohorts, indicating that the cut-offs were not overly inclusive. Subsequently, to find out which genes the identified DETs and DUTs correspond to, a Biomart export file containing all human gene and transcript IDs was downloaded from Ensembl Biomart, and the genes corresponding to the DETs and DUTs were identified. In order to interpret the gene lists obtained from the three analyses based on the analysis of the ROSMAP and MSBB datasets, the gene lists from DEGs, DETs and DUTs were combined for each dataset. Then, functional enrichment analysis was applied to the two combined lists via g:Profiler (Kolberg et al. 2023 ). Functional enrichment analysis was performed using g:Profiler with default parameters unless otherwise stated. Briefly, enrichment was assessed against the g:SCS multiple testing correction method, using a significance threshold of adjusted p -value &lt; 0.05. The organism was set to Homo sapiens, and all annotated genes were used as the background gene set. Evaluation and Integration of Differential Gene and Transcript Analysis Results In the combined list of DEG, DET and DUT genes from each dataset, the origin of the genes (i.e., from which analysis they were derived) was represented with a scoring system. According to this scoring system, genes identified in only one of the three analyses (DEG, DET, DUT) were scored as ‘1’, those identified in two different analyses were scored as ‘2’, and those identified in each of the three analyses were scored as ‘3’. Then, these scores for ROSMAP and MSBB were combined, such as R1-M2, R2-M3, etc. In other words, R1-M2 designated the case where the gene was identified in one analysis in ROSMAP and two analyses in MSBB. Genes with such scores were integrated into a single categorised final gene list, reflecting both cross-dataset and cross-analysis evidence. The resulting genes were then compared with two different gene lists from the literature. The first list is the AD-related gene list created by Hu et al. (Hu et al. 2017). They screened 823 genetic association studies from PubMed, and they obtained a total of 430 AD-related human genes. The second list, the Learning-Memory-Cognition (LMC) related gene list, was created in this study from different sources by collecting genes from various articles (Aimone et al. 2014 ; Bajaffer et al. 2021 ; Copping et al. 2017 ; Fan et al. 2019 ; Kandel 2012 ; Levy et al. 2022 ; Savitz et al. 2006 ; Seto et al. 2021 ; Xia et al. 2021 ) and identifying genes related to learning, memory, or cognition from Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment terms and selecting human genes from the Genes to Cognition Database (genes2cognition.org) (Croning et al. 2009 ). With the combination of these sources, the list includes 1542 LMC-related genes (Supplementary Information File 2 ). These two gene lists were separately compared with the final scored gene list from this study and evaluated for their significance in AsymAD. To capture all functional pathways associated with the candidate genes, g:Profiler was run by setting the p -value threshold to 1 in the enrichment analysis, ensuring the inclusion of all pathways regardless of statistical significance. Pathways containing at least three candidate genes and previously associated with AsymAD or AD-related cognitive decline were selected for visualisation. Key selected pathways and their corresponding genes were visualised using a Sankey plot, which was created using the SRplot web tool (Tang et al. 2023 ). Subnetwork Analysis A subnetwork analysis was performed to inspect the interactions between the scored genes that were common in both datasets (See Methods Sect. 2.5 ), and a subnetwork containing the associations of these genes was created from a human protein-protein interaction (PPI) network. The KeyPathwayMiner (version 5.0) package (Alcaraz et al. 2014 ) was used for all of the subnetwork analyses. A human PPI network was obtained from BioGRID (BioGRID Release 4.4.236). Only interactions between human proteins were selected, and self-loops and duplicate edges within the PPI network were cleaned, leading to a network of 20,096 proteins and 918,196 interactions. The applied parameters for the KeyPathwayMiner (KPM) based subnetwork analysis were INES for the strategy, GREEDY for the algorithm, and 8 for the K parameter. The K parameter shows the allowed maximum number of non-differential genes in the subnetwork to be discovered. Cytoscape (Shannon et al. 2003 ) was used to visualise the networks and interpret the results. Further Investigation with a Mouse Dataset For the ROSMAP/MSBB-based candidate genes that did not overlap with the LMC-related and AD-related gene lists, further analysis was conducted with an independent mouse microarray dataset. GSE147434 (Pérez-González et al. 2020 ) is a microarray dataset that includes the comparison of AD mice hippocampi between memory-impaired and memory-intact mice, where memory impairment was determined by behavioural tests such as the Morris Water Maze and Fear Conditioning tests. The mouse DEGs were identified (Barrett et al. 2013) using GEO2R (Clough et al. 2024 ) with a p -value threshold of 0.05, and human gene homologs of the mouse DEGs were obtained through Ensembl Biomart. The mouse DEG analysis was conducted in an exploratory context using an unadjusted p -value threshold ( p &lt; 0.05) due to limited sample size, yielding 2.4% significant genes. Enrichment analysis of the human homologs of the mouse genes was then performed via g:Profiler with standard parameters to reveal relevant biological pathways of the mouse DEGs. Next, the mouse genes were compared with the candidate genes from ROSMAP/MSBB to identify any shared genes between these two gene lists. To further investigate the relationship between the candidate genes and identified mouse DEGs, the lists of all associated transcription factors (TRANSFAC), microRNAs (miRTarBase), and biological pathways (KEGG, Reactome, WikiPathways) were identified for both candidate genes and mouse genes separately. g:Profiler was used for this by setting the p -value threshold to 1 in the analysis. Then, we checked if the ROSMAP/MSBB-based candidate genes and the mouse DEGs were regulated by the same TFs or miRNAs, or if they functioned within the same pathway. The comparison was performed using Jaccard similarity for each of the three categories (TFs, miRNA, pathway). To evaluate whether the observed overlaps in annotation space between the human and mouse candidate gene sets could arise by chance, we tested the significance of overlaps using Fisher’s exact test. For each category (TFs, miRNAs, and pathways), we extracted the unique term name entries associated with each gene set from the corresponding g:Profiler annotation outputs. Finally, interactions in the human PPI network (from Sect. 2.6 ) were used to reveal whether the candidate genes from ROSMAP/MSBB physically interact with mouse DEGs at the protein level. Cross-Species Functional Context of Candidate Genes Using a Mouse Dataset In order to further validate our candidate genes, a transcriptome dataset obtained from mouse models representing AD and AsymAD was analysed in this section (Fig. 6 a). In the dataset ( GSE147434 ) (Pérez-González et al. 2020 ), 696 DEGs were obtained by comparing gene expressions of memory-impaired and memory-intact mice with AD pathology. The 696 genes were enriched in signalling, response to stress, apoptotic process, and most importantly, sensory perception, which is consistent with behavioural assessments such as the Morris Water Maze and Fear Conditioning tests used in the study. We checked the relationship between 230 candidate genes identified in the previous section and the differential genes from the mouse dataset. In the 230 gene list, 45 genes intersected with AD-related or LMC-related genes (See Fig. 3 b). However, the remaining 185 genes may also play a role in progressing from AsymAD to AD, offering novel molecular insights into AsymAD. Therefore, we focused on the list of 185 genes for validation. Among the 185 genes that were not AD-related or LMC-related, four ( SLC6A12 , UXS1 , APOL3 and SPATA13 ) matched with the 696 DEGs from the mouse dataset. These genes may serve as novel biomarker genes for AsymAD. We next evaluated the 185 genes and 696 mouse DEGs in terms of their regulatory elements and biological pathways in order to identify potential shared mechanisms. Although only a small number of the 185 candidate genes overlapped with the mouse DEGs, we showed that these two gene sets are regulated by the same transcription factors (TFs), linked to the same microRNAs, and involved in the same biological pathways (Fig. 6 b). The Jaccard similarity analysis shows that the TFs of the two gene sets are 90% similar, miRNAs are 60% similar, and related pathways are 43% similar (Fig. 6 b). Moreover, Fisher’s exact tests confirmed that these overlaps in all three categories are highly significant ( p &lt; 2.2 × 10⁻¹⁶). We further checked if there were any known physical interactions between the proteins of the 185 genes and the DEGs from the mouse dataset. As a result, 71 of the 185 genes had physical interactions at the protein level with 141 of the mouse DEGs (Supplementary Table 6 , Fig. 6 c). Our analysis shows that even if the overlap between these gene sets is limited, the majority of the 185 genes either have direct physical interactions with the mouse DEGs share the same TF or miRNA regulators, or are involved in the same pathways. Then, the results of DEG, DET, and DUT analyses, comparing the AsymAD and AD groups, were integrated to identify candidate biomarker genes and reveal the underlying molecular mechanisms in AsymAD. Scoring the results of differential analyses has resulted in 230 candidate genes across both datasets. These 230 candidate genes were compared with the LMC-related and AD-related gene lists. The two candidate genes that were shared in both of these lists are NRXN3 and DGKB . The NRXN3 gene, which plays a role in synaptic cell adhesion and neurotransmitter release, has previously been associated with synaptic dysfunction with lower expression in AD (Zheng et al. 2018 ). Similarly, the results in this study showed that NRXN3 expression was lower in AD compared to AsymAD. Lower expression of this gene can interrupt synaptic integrity. The second gene, DGKB , is related to lipid and glycerolipid metabolism. DGKB regulates diacylglycerol (DAG) signalling by converting DAG to phosphatidic acid (PA). This process is crucial for synaptic plasticity processes such as long-term potentiation and long-term depression mechanisms that underlie learning and memory. Loss of DGKB activity results in decreased dendritic spine density and impairs synaptic function and synaptic plasticity (Lee et al. 2016 ). DGKB expression was identified to be lower in AD than in AsymAD in this study, and it demonstrates that higher expressions of this gene might be related to synaptic plasticity, hence neuroprotection. The GNG4 gene was identified as DET and DUT for both ROSMAP and MSBB (Fig. 4 ). GNG4 (G protein subunit gamma 4) is associated with pathways such as neurotransmitter receptors and postsynaptic signal transmission. Bonham et al. ( 2018 ) suggested that GNG4 may be related to cognitive decline during normal ageing. In that study, GNG4 showed relatively higher expression in the frontal cortex. GNG4 expression was reported to decrease with advancing age (Bonham et al. 2018 ). In another study, significant transcriptional changes in synaptic function-related genes were observed, and the GNG4 gene was downregulated in 12-month-old 5XFAD mice (Bouter et al. 2014 ). Bayraktar et al. ( 2021 ) investigated the molecular mechanisms of AD through network analysis, and extensive dysregulation of synapse-associated genes was observed in AD. The results showed that GNG4 was downregulated in the prefrontal cortex (Bayraktar et al. 2021 ). Interestingly, in the results obtained in this study, the overall expression of GNG4 was not significantly changed in the DEG analysis. Yet, one of its transcripts, GNG4-201 (ENST00000366597), is upregulated in AD in the MSBB dataset compared to AsymAD, and also the proportion of that transcript is higher in the AD for both datasets (Fig. 4 ). The fact that this transcript is upregulated in AD within the MSBB dataset and the transcript proportion increases in both datasets in a compatible manner suggests that the increase in the expression or proportion of this isoform of the GNG4 gene may be associated with cognitive decline during the transition from AsymAD to AD. The MRPL1 gene was identified as DET and DUT in both ROSMAP and MSBB datasets, and it was not DEG in either dataset (Fig. 5 ). The MRPL1 (Mitochondrial Ribosomal Protein L1) gene is a nuclear-derived gene that codes for a component of the large ribosomal subunit (39 S) of mitochondria (Elsadany et al. 2021 ). In recent years, the role of mitochondrial ribosomal proteins in neurodegenerative diseases has received attention, and some (e.g., MRPL44 ) have been linked to AD pathology (Del Giudice et al. 2023 ). A meta-analysis combining different brain samples found that genes such as MRPL1 , MRPL13 , and MRPL15 had markedly lower expression in AD brains compared to healthy controls (Brooks and Mias 2019 ). Similarly, in another machine learning-based study, MRPL1 was highlighted to be lowly expressed in the late-onset AD and mild cognitive impairment (MCI) groups compared to healthy controls (Fernández-Martínez et al. 2020 ). In our case, MRPL1-201 (ENST00000315567) in the ROSMAP dataset had lower expression in AD, while the usage of the MRPL1-203 (ENST00000504901) transcript increased in AD compared to AsymAD. In the MSBB dataset, MRPL1-206 (ENST00000515625) had lower expression in AD, while the usage of this transcript decreased in AD compared to AsymAD. The functional and protein-coding canonical transcript MRPL1-201 showed lower expression in AD relative to AsymAD, indicating reduced mitochondrial ribosomal activity, consistent with the literature. In contrast, alternative transcripts (e.g., MRPL1-203 and MRPL1-206 ) that did not produce proteins or whose coding sequence was not identified exhibited different usage profiles in disease status. This suggests that transcript balance of this gene is disturbed in AD. In this context, MRPL1 and similar mitochondrial ribosomal proteins are part of the mitochondria-induced biological changes observed in AD and may be involved in the molecular signature of the disease. The ADAMTS2 gene was identified as DEG and DET for ROSMAP and as DEG, DET, and DUT for MSBB (Supplementary Fig. 5 ). A disintegrin-like and metalloproteinase with thrombospondin type 1 motif (ADAMTS) is a family of extracellular matrix (ECM) proteases (Rose et al. 2021 ). There is evidence that reelin, known to regulate neurogenesis, synapse formation, and synaptic plasticity positively, is degraded by ADAMTS and that decreased reelin levels may increase tau phosphorylation (Gurses et al. 2016 ). A study involving the generation of ADAMTS2 knockout mice found that ADAMTS2 significantly contributes to the proteolytic cleavage and inactivation of reelin in the postnatal cerebral cortex and hippocampus (Yamakage et al. 2019 ). In addition, another study using the ROSMAP cohort found that high expression of ADAMTS2 accelerated cognitive decline in episodic memory and global cognitive functions (McCorkindale et al. 2022 ). In the results of this study, the global expression of the ADAMTS2 gene, classified within the R2-M3 group, increased in both datasets in AD compared to AsymAD. The gene was detected in the MSBB dataset as DEG, DET, and DUT and the ROSMAP dataset as DEG and DET, making it a robust candidate. In the DET case, it was observed that the expression of one of its isoforms, ADAMTS2-203 (ENST00000518335), increased in both datasets in the AD condition relative to AsymAD. In the DUT analysis case, its proportions increased within AD in ROSMAP (Supplementary Fig. 5 ). The lower activity of the ADAMTS2 gene, especially the ADAMTS2-203 transcript, may provide a neuroprotective effect in AsymAD. At the same time, its overexpression in AD may be directly associated with AD neuropathology. Another gene that was identified to be a common DUT between the two datasets (Supplementary Fig. 6 a) is the PCOLCE gene. The PCOLCE (procollagen C-endopeptidase enhancer 1) gene is a member of the collagen family gene located in the ECM. The study by Lepelletier et al. ( 2017 ) observed alterations in the expression of ECM molecules during the early stages of AD (Lepelletier et al. 2017 ). In addition, another study found that collagen-binding proteins, including PCOLCE , are dysregulated in the prefrontal cortex of AD patients (Williams et al. 2021 ). The protein-forming, intron-retained transcript of the PCOLCE gene, PCOLCE-205 (ENST00000472348), was downregulated in AsymAD compared to AD. This may prove that the reduction of this transcript, specific to AsymAD, has a protective effect. In addition, it is known that the ADAMTS2 and PCOLCE genes encode enzymes that modify procollagen molecules (Smagin et al. 2019 ). The fact that these two genes are collagen family genes emphasises the importance of this pathway, especially the role of these genes in AD. ENPP5 (Ectonucleotide Pyrophosphatase/Phosphodiesterase Family Member 5) is the least characterised ENPP family member. It has been suggested that it degrades nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD) and may have a potential role in NAD-based neurotransmission, and studies in rats indicated that the encoded protein may play a role in neuronal cell communication. A transcript isoform of the ENPP5 gene, ENPP5-201 (ENST00000230565), was found to have an increased proportion in the AsymAD group in both datasets compared to AD (Supplementary Fig. 6 b). The increased proportion of the ENPP5-201 transcript isoform in AsymAD compared to AD may be a compensatory response to balance NAD metabolism in the early stages of the disease. Since decreased NAD levels can accelerate neurodegenerative processes (Blacher et al. 2015 ), an increased proportion of ENPP5-201 in AsymAD may function as a response to oxidative stress or as an early cellular defence mechanism(Gong et al. 2013 ). However, in the later stages of AD, a decrease in the proportion may be associated with a general degradation of NAD metabolism, a change in the proportions of different transcript isoforms, or altered synaptic functions (Chaubey et al. 2022 ). Thus, while the increased proportion of ENPP5-201 in AsymAD may help maintain cellular homeostasis in the early stages of the disease, it may indicate that compensatory mechanisms become inadequate due to changes in the overall transcript balance in the later stages. Some protein family genes were also identified in the discovered subnetwork in Fig. 3 d. For example, the COL25A1 , COL26A1 , and PCOLCE genes belong to the same protein family, as they encode collagen family proteins (Smagin et al. 2019 ). PCOLCE may be a critical regulator that indirectly affects the functions of collagen family genes COL25A1 and COL26A1 . What these genes have in common is their effects on the structuring and organisation of the ECM. ECM has essential functions in the central nervous system, such as structural support, intercellular communication, and extracellular signalling. Abnormal accumulation and modifications of ECM components in AD may contribute to the formation of Aβ plaques and tau proteins and neurodegeneration processes through synaptic signal transmission, oxidative stress and inflammatory responses (Sun et al. 2021 ). Since COL25A1 plays an important role in the neurodegenerative processes (Tong et al. 2010 ), COL26A1 in the connective tissue stability (Bader et al. 2009 ), and PCOLCE in the maturation of procollagens (Steiglitz et al. 2006 ), dysfunction of these genes may lead to diseases like AD, related to the disruption of the integrity of the ECM. Also, the enrichment results show that the ‘collagen biosynthesis and modifying enzymes’ term is associated with the genes ADAMTS2 , COL26A1 , PCOLCE , BMP1 , and COL25A1 . Among these genes, PCOLCE , COL25A1 , and COL26A1 interacted with each other not directly but through the exception genes (Fig. 3 d), the genes that were allowed in the candidate gene subnetwork by the KPM algorithm, although they were not in the DEG, DET or DUT lists (Supplementary Fig. 8 ). The exception genes played a critical role in the subnetwork discovery by bridging the input genes. Another example of such related genes that are in the same protein family is MRPL1 and MRPL30 . They are genes that encode proteins associated with the large subunit of the mitochondrial ribosome. These genes play critical roles in mitochondrial protein synthesis and support cellular energy production. Disruptions in the function of mitochondrial ribosomal proteins are associated with oxidative stress and dysfunction in energy metabolism and may play a role in AD (Lunnon et al. 2017 ). Furthermore, MRPL30 is one of the genes validated by the mouse datasets used in this study. Additionally, cytosolic ribosome genes such as RPL19 , FAU , RPL29 , and RPL41 can serve as similar examples. Due to their ribosomal functions and critical roles in protein synthesis, these genes may contribute to oxidative stress, energy metabolism disorders, and cellular stress mechanisms in AD. Unbalanced expression of ribosomal proteins in the brain is associated with neuronal loss and dysfunction observed in AD (Suzuki et al. 2022 ). In this study, we categorised genes and transcripts based on their change profiles in Control-AsymAD and AsymAD-AD comparisons with the aim of better defining molecular transitions in AD. Our findings revealed that the identified candidate genes, such as MRPL1 , ADAMTS2 , GNG4 , PCOLCE , and ENPP5 and most of the other genes in the list showed significant changes specifically during the transition from AsymAD to AD while remaining stable at the Control-AsymAD stage. This specific pattern of change supports our hypothesis that these genes are primarily involved in cognitive resilience mechanisms, rather than being early markers of general AD pathology. In the list of 230 candidate genes, 45 of them showed an intersection with AD-related or LMC-related genes. However, 185 of them may also be important in the progression from AsymAD to AD (Fig. 3 b). Therefore, a transcriptome dataset obtained from a mouse model representing AD and AsymAD was additionally analysed. The comparison of candidate genes with the mouse dataset also enabled the discovery of genes that were not available in the AD-related or LMC-related gene lists. For example, SLC6A12 , mentioned above in the context of AD-related studies, was also identified by the mouse dataset. SLC6A12 is a subfamily of gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA), and the upregulation of this gene, accompanying the downregulation of GABA signalling, has been associated with cognitive impairment in AD (Zhao et al. 2021 ). Another example is APOL3 (Apolipoprotein L3), which has not been directly implicated as a strong risk gene in AD, but its immune and inflammation-related functions may be related to neuroinflammation mechanisms in AD progression (L. Wang et al., 2024 ). Moreover, it has been noted that various apolipoproteins, including APOL3 , showed expression changes in AD brains, promoting their role in AD progression (Elliott et al. 2010 ). SPATA13 is a Rho GTPase guanine nucleotide exchange factor, and a direct association with AD has not been confirmed in current studies. However, it has been shown to influence neuronal functions and specifically regulate social behaviour and activity levels in mouse models (Bourbia et al. 2019 ). More recent transcriptomic analyses revealed that SPATA13 is prominent in gene networks associated with AD, although this association may be indirectly linked with lncRNAs involved in these networks through processes such as synaptic signalling and synaptic plasticity (Ng et al. 2024 ). Finally, UXS1 has not been identified as a strong risk gene in AD but is involved in AD-linked modules in gene networks specific to some ethnic populations (Dai et al. 2022 ) and shows lysosomal accumulation in APOE4 -expressing cells, suggesting the possibility of indirect contribution to AD pathophysiology (Krogsaeter et al. 2023 ). Additionally, in our findings, the USX1 gene, which is found in both the mouse ortholog gene list and the 185-candidate gene list, exists in the interaction network in Fig. 6 c, supporting the findings about this gene. Furthermore, it was observed that the genes from the mouse dataset and 185 candidate genes were remarkably similar in terms of common TFs, miRNAs and pathways (Fig. 6 b), and some genes in these two gene sets interacted with each other through the PPI network (Fig. 6 c). Overall, these findings suggest functional and regulatory concordance related to cognitive status differences observed between memory-impaired and memory-intact states within the AD–AsymAD framework. A limitation of this study is the inconsistencies observed across the two studied datasets in both the number of DEGs, DETs, and DUTs and their regulation levels due to differences between the datasets and the analytical methods used. As a suggestion for future directions, it is envisaged that cellular heterogeneity can be examined in detail by using scRNA-seq AD datasets after identifying individuals with AsymAD using neuropathological and clinical scores, as presented in this study. Single-cell-level analyses can help us understand which cell groups are more affected in AD and AsymAD states or which pathways are activated specifically for cell types by revealing transcriptomic differences between the cell types. In addition, the AsymAD mouse dataset used in this study only allowed identification of DEGs since it was a microarray-based transcriptome dataset. RNA-Seq-based data from memory-impaired and memory-intact mouse models will enable more detailed comparisons and validation. Conclusions The findings of this study provided new insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying AsymAD by integrating DEG, DET, and DUT analyses in two well-established AD datasets. Several candidate genes that may play critical roles in synaptic function, ECM integrity, and mitochondrial energy metabolism were identified. In particular, NRXN3 and DGKB were highlighted as potential biomarker genes due to their involvement in synaptic plasticity and neurotransmitter release and their altered expression patterns in AD and AsymAD. Additionally, the role of ADAMTS2 in ECM dysregulation and its association with cognitive decline were highlighted, along with the importance of certain collagen-related genes ( PCOLCE , COL25A1 , COL26A1 , ADAMTS2 ) in maintaining ECM integrity. New findings on GNG4 and its regulation at the transcript level suggest its role in synapse-related molecular mechanisms in AD progression. Furthermore, the increased ratio of ENPP5 isoform in AsymAD may point to early neuroprotective or compensatory mechanisms associated with NAD-based neurotransmission. Finally, dysregulation of mitochondrial ribosomal genes such as MRPL1 and MRPL30 , critical for energy metabolism, further highlights the interplay of mitochondrial dysfunction in AD pathology. In addition, a striking mechanism in the comparison of AD vs. AsymAD is synaptic plasticity, which plays a role in memory formation and the long-term potentiation mechanism, where synapses are strengthened due to active use. These may be the most extensive mechanisms involved in the protection of AsymAD individuals from cognitive decline. Thus, despite the presence of pathology in AsymAD individuals, the main reason for the absence of cognitive impairment could be attributed to the strong synaptic plasticity and signal transduction between synapses.
