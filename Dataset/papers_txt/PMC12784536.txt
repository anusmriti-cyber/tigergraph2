Tau pathology is a defining feature of Alzheimer's disease (AD), with hyperphosphorylated Tau (p‑Tau) emerging as a central biomarker for early diagnosis and disease monitoring. Various p‑Tau epitopes have demonstrated superior diagnostic precision and now form the molecular basis of updated AD diagnostic frameworks. Classical immunoassays such as enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), chemiluminescent enzyme immunoassay (CLEIA), and single molecule array (SIMOA) remain central to fluid based detection, offering high sensitivity and clinical validation. Recent advances in tau biology, especially in post-translational modifications, have driven the development of next generation biosensors. Electrochemical, optical, and nanostructured platforms now enable real-time, label-free, and attomolar level detection of p‑Tau in biofluids and live cell models. These systems are increasingly portable and suitable for point of care or in vivo applications. This review highlights the evolution of p‑Tau detection technologies, from benchmark immunoassays to cutting edge biosensors. Special attention is given to advanced affinity reagents, including aptamers, synthetic peptides, and antibody mimetics, which enhance biosensor specificity, stability, and translational potential. Together, these innovations are redefining AD diagnostics, enabling early intervention and more effective disease monitoring. Affecting over 55 million individuals worldwide, with this number projected to nearly triple by 2050. AD represents a growing global crisis, placing a tremendous social and economic burden on patients, families, and healthcare systems [ 3 ]. Pathologically, the disease is marked by the extracellular accumulation of amyloid- β 1–42 (Aβ) plaques and the intracellular formation of neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs), primarily composed of hypermodified and aggregated tau protein [ 4 ]. In recent years, research has increasingly focused on tau pathology, especially the role of post-translational modifications (PTMs) in altering Tau's structural and functional properties. PTMs, particularly hyperphosphorylation, drive conformational changes that weaken Tau's microtubule-binding capacity, leading to its mislocalization, aggregation, and ultimately, neurotoxicity. Notably, tau hyperphosphorylation correlates more closely with neuronal loss and cognitive decline than Aβ deposition, underscoring its central role in AD pathogenesis [ 5 , 6 ]. Beyond phosphorylation, additional PTMs, including acetylation, ubiquitination, and SUMOylation, also contribute significantly to Tau's pathological transformation. Acetylation at key lysine residues (K274 and K281) impairs Tau's microtubule binding capacity, disrupts mitochondrial dynamics, and induces apoptotic pathways [ 7 , 8 ]. Acetylation within the microtubule binding repeat domain (K280/K311) promotes filament nucleation and has been confirmed in AD brain tissue using site-specific antibodies [ 9 ]. Ubiquitination, particularly within repeat domains, becomes more prevalent in late stage tauopathy, where it regulates tau degradation and competes with acetylation for lysine residues [ 10 ]. Additionally, SUMOylation at K340 has been shown to promote pathological phosphorylation and impair ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis, thereby stabilizing toxic tau species [ 8 ]. Despite the complexity of tau PTMs, hyperphosphorylated Tau (p-Tau) has emerged as the most reliable biomarker for early, specific, and dynamic diagnosis of AD. Among the phospho-epitopes, p-Tau181, p-Tau217, and p-Tau231 show superior diagnostic performance, offering enhanced sensitivity, staging resolution, and disease specificity compared to total Tau or Aβ [ 11 , 12 ]. However, current clinical approaches, including cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis and positron emission tomography (PET) imaging, are invasive, costly, and not widely accessible, limiting their suitability for early- early-stage detection or longitudinal screening [ 13 ]. To address these challenges, biosensors targeting phosphorylated Tau have emerged as promising tools for next-generation diagnostics. By integrating high affinity molecular recognition elements such as antibodies, aptamers, or engineered peptides with sensitive transduction platforms based on optical, electrochemical, or nanomaterial interfaces, these biosensors enable noninvasive, real, and precise detection of p-Tau at clinically relevant concentrations [ 14 – 21 ]. This review highlights recent advances in tau biology, focusing on phosphorylation as a critical biomarker in AD. We further explore cutting-edge biosensor technologies designed to detect p‑Tau with precision, showcasing the convergence of protein engineering, synthetic biology, and nanotechnology. These platforms offer scalable, point of care diagnostics with the potential to transform early detection and personalized management of neurodegenerative diseases. Tau protein biology and isoforms Tau is a neuronal microtubule-associated protein (MAP) encoded by the MAPT gene, expressed predominantly in neurons of the central nervous system [ 22 ]. Alternative splicing of exons 2, 3, and 10 generates six primary isoforms in adults, distinguished by the presence of either three (3R) or four (4R) microtubule binding repeats and 0–2 N-terminal inserts (Fig. 1 ). These isoforms share common functional domains, including an acidic N-terminus, one proline rich region, the repeat domain for microtubule binding, and a C-terminal tail, but differ in their microtubule affinity and intracellular distribution [ 23 , 24 ]. Likewise, the four repeat (4R) variants bind more avidly to microtubules and show broader somatodendritic distribution than the three repeat (3R) forms [ 25 ]. During development, the fetal brain expresses almost exclusively fetal Tau, the highly hyperphosphorylated 0N3R isoform; post-natal maturation to a 1:1 3R:4R ratio is accompanied by global dephosphorylation, highlighting a developmental role for phosphoregulation [ 26 – 28 ]. The longest human isoform harbours ~ 79 potential Ser/Thr phosphosites that act as rheostats for axonal transport and cytoskeletal stability [ 3 ]. However, pathological processes such as aberrant hyperphosphorylation within the proline rich and repeat domains disrupt its binding to microtubules, leading to misfolding, oligomerization, and aggregation into paired helical filaments (PHFs) and neurofibrillary tangles, hallmark features of Alzheimer's disease and related tauopathies [ 29 – 31 ]. Cryo-EM has resolved Alzheimer's specific Tau filament structures purified from AD brain tissue, revealing structural polymorphism and a prion-like mode of propagation [ 32 ]. Fig. 1 Tau protein biology and isoforms. The human MAPT gene undergoes alternative splicing of exons 2, 3, and 10 to generate six brain-specific tau isoforms (0N3R, 1N3R, 2N3R, 0N4R, 1N4R, and 2N4R), ranging from 352 to 441 amino acids. These isoforms differ by the presence of zero, one, or two N-terminal inserts (encoded by exons 2 and 3) and by containing either three (3R) or four (4R) microtubule-binding repeats (regulated by exon 10). Tau is structurally organized into four main domains: the N-terminal projection domain, which extends away from microtubules and interacts with plasma membrane components; the proline-rich region, which engages in intracellular signaling via Src-family kinases like Fyn; the microtubule-binding domain, essential for microtubule polymerization and stability; and the C-terminal domain, which also contributes to microtubule dynamics and membrane interactions. Tau isoform expression is developmentally regulated, where 0N3R is predominant in the fetal brain, while all six isoforms are expressed in the adult brain. In adults, 3R and 4R isoforms are expressed at roughly equal levels, with 0 N, 1 N, and 2 N isoforms comprising approximately 37%, 54%, and 9% of total Tau, respectively Experimental or genetic shifts that disturb the 3R/4R balance accelerate aggregation and neurotoxicity [ 28 , 33 ], whereas the recently discovered human-specific W-Tau isoform, produced by intron-12 retention, shows reduced aggregation and even inhibits Aβ and Tau fibrillization in vitro [ 34 , 35 ]. Multi-omics and proximity labelling now place Tau at the center of RNA metabolism, mitochondrial bioenergetics, and synaptic-vesicle cycling networks that are perturbed early in disease [ 36 , 37 ]. Hyperphosphorylated Tau in Alzheimer's disease Tau, a neuronal microtubule-associated protein, plays a central role in maintaining cytoskeletal integrity and axonal transport. In Alzheimer's disease (AD), Tau becomes abnormally hyperphosphorylated, particularly within its proline-rich and microtubule-binding repeat domains. This modification reduces Tau's affinity for microtubules, destabilizing the cytoskeletal network and promoting conformational changes that favor misfolding, oligomerization, and aggregation into paired helical filaments (PHFs) and neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs), pathological hallmarks of AD and other tauopathies [ 38 – 41 ], (Fig. 2 ). P‑Tau exerts widespread deleterious effects on neuronal function by forming neurofibrillary tangles, destabilizing microtubules, and promoting mitochondrial dysfunction, calcium dysregulation, and synaptic degeneration. It also triggers lipid metabolism imbalance and activates neuroinflammatory pathways, collectively driving the progression of neurodegeneration in Alzheimer's disease While Tau's pathological transformation has traditionally been linked to axonal dysfunction and neurodegeneration, growing evidence reveals that Tau exerts profound effects on presynaptic nanomechanics and vesicle dynamics. P‑Tau mislocalizes to synaptic terminals where it binds to the vesicle membrane protein synaptogyrin-3 and crosslinks synaptic vesicles with F-actin, creating a rigid vesicle mesh that hinders vesicle mobility and impairs neurotransmitter release [ 42 , 43 ]. These events disrupt short-term plasticity and contribute to early cognitive deficits. Mechanistically, when Tau becomes hyperphosphorylated, it acquires a toxic gain-of-function that alters its normal interactome. In particular, p-Tau mislocalizes to presynaptic terminals and aberrantly binds to core components of the vesicle fusion machinery. Studies using phospho-positive Tau (AT8⁺/PHF1⁺) in neuronal models have demonstrated that p-Tau directly associates with syntaxin-1, VAMP2, and synaptogyrin-3 at the presynaptic membrane, creating a rigid vesicle–actin network that impairs vesicle mobility and interferes with SNARE-mediated docking and fusion [ 42 , 44 ]. In neurons expressing pathogenic, AT8-positive Tau (P301L), Tau aberrantly interacts with the Hsc70/CSPα chaperone system, which normally stabilizes SNAP25 in a release-competent state, thereby reducing SNARE availability and impairing calcium-triggered neurotransmitter release [ 37 , 45 ]. At the level of calcium signaling, hyperphosphorylated Tau disrupts ER-mitochondrial interactions and sensitizes calcium channels, increasing cytosolic Ca 2+ transients. These changes alter the probability and synchronicity of synaptic vesicle release, further perturbing information flow across neuronal networks [ 46 , 47 ]. P- Tau also impairs mitochondrial positioning and ATP production at the synapse, creating energetic deficits that impair vesicle loading and recycling. In hTau mouse models, p-Tau preferentially associates with synaptic mitochondria, reducing respiration and ATP generation. Similarly, in human AD cortical tissue, presynaptic terminals exhibit fewer and damaged mitochondria, correlating with elevated p-Tau levels and a diminished vesicle pool size [ 48 , 49 ]. p-Tau disrupts endocytic pathways by sequestering dynamin-1, thereby blocking clathrin-mediated vesicle retrieval and reducing the availability of vesicles for sustained neurotransmission. In parallel, p-Tau-stabilized actin bundles increase cytoskeletal rigidity, further impeding vesicle mobilization from the reserve pool, ultimately leading to synaptic fatigue and impaired circuit resilience [ 50 , 51 ]. Further, extracellular p-Tau oligomers propagate synaptic dysfunction. They are internalized by neurons and misdirected to postsynaptic sites, where they interact with scaffolding proteins such as PSD-95 and kinases like FYN to aberrantly stabilize NMDA receptors. This impairs glutamate signaling, disrupts dendritic spine morphology, and accelerates synaptic degeneration [ 44 , 52 ]. Additionally, there is a bidirectional relationship between tau hyperphosphorylation and lipid metabolism in Alzheimer's disease (AD). P-Tau accumulates in cholesterol- and sphingolipid-rich lipid rafts within neuronal membranes, where kinases such as CDK5 and GSK‑3β become activated, further driving tau phosphorylation and mislocalization to the plasma membrane [ 53 , 54 ]. Conversely, lipid dysregulation, particularly increased ceramide levels, can activate GSK‑3β, contributing directly to Tau hyperphosphorylation and promoting aggregation via the PHF6 motifs. Such alterations in lipid composition are observed early in tauopathy, disrupting membrane trafficking, signaling pathways, and organelles, thereby exacerbating tau pathology and neurodegeneration [ 55 ]. Hyperphosphorylated Tau as a diagnostic biomarker The p‑Tau biomarker story began in the mid-1990s, when epitope mapping of Alzheimer's paired-helical filaments revealed that monoclonal antibodies AT270 and AT180 target Tau hyperphosphorylated at Thr181 and Thr231, respectively, establishing the first disease relevant sites for fluid assays [ 56 ]. Within a few years, CSF sandwich ELISAs built around these epitopes showed that p-Tau181 rose markedly in symptomatic AD, while p-Tau231 shifted even earlier and differentiated AD from frontotemporal and other dementias; in mild cognitive impairment cohorts, CSF p-Tau231 increases tracked prodromal decline better than p-Tau181 [ 57 ]. These two sites, together with total Tau and Aβ 42, formed the core "A/T/N" CSF panel that underpins today's biological AD criteria. The past decade moved Tau phosphorylation markers from CSF to blood. An ultrasensitive Simoa immunoassay first showed that plasma p-Tau181 could distinguish AD from controls in 2017, proving peripheral Tau fragments mirror central pathology [ 58 ]. The real breakthrough came in 2020, when multi‑cohort studies demonstrated that plasma p‑Tau217 exhibits a four to six fold AD-specific increase and outperforms p‑Tau181 for amyloid and Tau‑PET concordance [ 12 ]. Subsequent longitudinal work revealed that p-Tau231 is the ultra-early blood signal, rising while amyloid is still sub-threshold on PET, thereby flagging pre-symptomatic individuals for prevention trials [ 59 , 60 ]. Finally, fully automated multiplex panels released in 2023 combine Aβ₄₂/₄₀, p‑Tau181, and p‑Tau217 (with optional ApoE4) on clinical immunochemistry analyzers, achieving AUC ≈ 0.90–0.93 for detecting brain amyloidosis and forecasting conversion within six years [ 61 ]. Some other hyperphosphorylated epitopes have been identified even earlier, starting with the identification of abnormally modified Tau species in neurofibrillary tangles in the 1990s. Monoclonal antibodies such as AT8 (targeting Ser202/Thr205), PHF-1 (Ser396/404), and CP13 (pSer202) were instrumental in both post-mortem staging (Braak staging) and experimental modeling of Tau pathology [ 62 ]. These sites, particularly pSer396/404, were among the earliest identified as hyperphosphorylated in AD brain tissue, with pSer396 emerging prior to tangle formation in some models [ 63 – 65 ]. Yet despite their histological importance, most of these epitopes proved less robust for fluid-based assays due to low signal-to-noise ratio, limited dynamic range, or poor specificity in early clinical cohorts [ 66 ]. Efforts to detect pSer396, pSer404, or pSer202 in CSF have shown some promise, but none matched the diagnostic precision or staging potential of p-T181, p-T217, or p-T231, which are now considered the most clinically actionable sites [ 67 , 68 ]. Among recently characterized phosphorylation sites, pSer381 has gained attention as a late-stage marker linked to mature neurofibrillary tangles, irreversible tau aggregation, and neuronal degeneration. Similar to pSer396/404, its current utility remains primarily in mechanistic, postmortem, or seeding-activity studies, rather than in clinical biomarker workflows. In contrast, p-T231, once thought to be redundant with p-T181, has re-emerged as high-sensitivity, an ultra-early blood-based biomarker in the preclinical phase of AD. Across multiple recent cohorts, p-T231 becomes abnormal before global Aβ-PET positivity, particularly in medial orbitofrontal, precuneus, and posterior cingulate regions. At the next stage of pathology, p-T217 hyperphosphorylated selectively in AD and largely spared in non-AD tauopathies—shows the strongest correlations with Aβ-PET, tau-PET, and longitudinal clinical progression. Evidence from both single-center and multicenter studies consistently indicates that p-T217 provides the largest fold-change and the highest diagnostic accuracy among blood-based tau biomarkers [ 12 , 47 , 59 , 69 ]. These advances define a biomarker cascade p-T231 → p-T217 → p-T181 now central to both diagnosis and disease staging. At the same time, other historically essential epitopes like pSer202, pSer396, and pSer404 continue to inform histopathological analysis, Tau seeding assays, and mechanistic studies of tau aggregation. In contrast, p-tau217 remains the most robust marker across platforms, with multiple studies reporting AUCs consistently in the mid-0.9 range [ 80 ] Incremental diagnostic value of combining p-Tau with Aβ42/40, NfL, GFAP Plasma p-tau particularly p-tau217 has emerged as the strongest single blood based correlate of AD pathology, with AUC values typically ranging from ~ 0.90 to 0.95 across multiple cohorts [ 80 ]. However, contemporary evidence from 2023–2025 shows that combining p-tau with an amyloid metric (Aβ42/40), astroglial response (NfL, GFAP) improves diagnostic accuracy, risk prediction, and clinical decision benefit beyond any individual marker [ 81 ]. Across multi-cohort comparative studies, the largest performance gains appear when p-tau217 is paired with Aβ42/40, especially in cognitively unimpaired and early MCI stages. For example, kang et al. employed Simoa/MSD assays to quantify plasma p-tau217 together with Aβ42/40, GFAP, and NfL to assess the diagnostic performance of biomarker combinations for Aβ PET positivity. They demonstrated that combination of p-tau217 and Aβ42/40 significantly improved diagnostic accuracy (ΔAUC: ~ 0.03–0.10) with net reclassification/decision-curve analyses confirming clinical net benefit for panels in triage workflows compared to p-tau217 alone with the greatest effect in cognitively unimpaired individuals [ 82 ]. Likewise, xiao et al. reported that combinations of p-tau181 + Aβ42/40 achieved an AUC of ~ 0.83 for predicting clinical progression in amnestic MCI, underscoring the prognostic performance of multi analyte panels in community and clinical settings [ 83 ]. Furthermore, adding markers of neurodegeneration or astroglial injury like NfL or GFAP to a p-tau ± Aβ panel improves prediction of short-term clinical progression and raises positive predictive values (PPVs) to ~ 43% in community-based cohorts. Net reclassification indices and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) indicate meaningful reclassification for at risk individuals and reflecting reduced unnecessary PET referrals [ 84 ]. Earlier work supports these patterns: adding NfL or GFAP generally yields modest ΔAUC improvements ≤ (0.01–0.05) for predicting cognitive decline. Palmqvist et al. further demonstrated that combining Aβ42/Aβ40, p-Tau181, and ApoE4 improved the AUCs significantly (0.90—0.93) from AUC of 0.83–0.87 of Aβ42/Aβ40 for discriminating Aβ-positive versus Aβ-negative. p-Tau181, p-Tau217 and ApoE4 predicted AD dementia within 6 years in CU (AUC 0.88) and p-Tau181, p-Tau217, and Aβ42/Aβ40 in MCI (AUC 0.87) [ 61 ]. Thus, a panel of biomarkers may have superior Aβ-/+ status predictive capability across the AD continuum [ 85 ]. Operationally, single analyte immunoassays on Simoa or Lumipulse platforms require around 0.5–1 mL plasma and offer turnaround within hours to 24—48 h depending on lab work flow; multiplex immunoassays using the same platform maintain similar time, whereas cross-platform panels incorporating LC–MS/MS increase sample volume needs (often &gt; 1 ml for immunoprecipitation) and extend processing times to days. Cost profiles follow the same pattern: single immunoassays generally fall in the low-moderate range, while 3–5-analyte laboratory panels-especially those involving MS rise to moderate-high cost [ 86 ]. In addition to immunoassay-based panels, biosensor technologies are making rapid strides. Electrochemical aptamer sensors targeting p-Tau231 have demonstrated pg/mL sensitivity in human serum and possess inherent multiplexing capacity [ 87 , 88 ]. Plasmonic nanobridge sensors (asymmetric PAN chips) offer attomolar detection sensitivity and have been used in small clinical pilot studies, with machine-learning models (SVM) classifying healthy, MCI, and AD subjects with high accuracy [ 89 , 90 ]. These platforms exemplify the potential to measure multiple biomarkers from minimal sample volumes in a single run. However, translation remains constrained by important real-world challenges: in native plasma, signal degradation, nonspecific adsorption (“protein corona”), baseline drift, and the need for antifouling coatings and matrix-matched calibration are recurring issues [ 91 ]. Rigorous EQA, lot bridging, and reporting of matrix rejection rates are essential for clinical deployment [ 109 ] Immunoassays First generation ELISA and fully automated chemiluminescent enzyme immunoassays (CLEIA), which detect target proteins via antibody capture followed by enzyme-mediated optical signal amplification, remain the workhorses for clinical cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and plasma testing (Fig. 4 A and C). A five-site study (multicenter) of 1,767 participants in the BioFINDER and AIBL cohorts showed that plasma p‑Tau217 and p‑Tau181 measured on a CLEIA (Lumipulse) platform separated Alzheimer's disease (AD) from non-AD neurodegenerative disorders with area under the curve (AUC) values of 0.90–0.95. CLEIA offers sub picogram per milliliter limits of detection and eight‑minute run times, yet its narrow optical dynamic range, susceptibility to blood‑matrix interference, and dependence on proprietary reagents keep cross‑laboratory harmonisation challenging [ 110 , 111 ]. Ultrasensitive digital platforms Advanced immunoassays such as Single Molecule Array (Simoa), which isolates and digitally counts individual immunocomplexes on femtoliter-sized beads, and electrochemiluminescence based assays (MSD), which rely on antibody-antigen binding detected via electrically stimulated light emission, enable femtogram level quantification of low abundance p‑Tau isoforms in plasma (Fig. 4 B). In a 2024 cross platform evaluation involving 734 participants, plasma p‑Tau217 measured by Simoa outperformed p‑Tau181 (AUC ~ 0.93) in identifying Aβ-PET positivity and tracking cognitive decline [ 112 , 113 ]. Despite their performance, these platforms are expensive, require centralized laboratory infrastructure, and suffer from inter-assay variability (coefficients of variation up to 20%), making them impractical for broad point of care deployment [ 114 ]. Mass spectrometry Antibody free parallel reaction monitoring (PRM) and targeted liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) quantify multiple phosphorylated sites such as p‑T217, p‑T231, and p‑S396 in a single analytical run by separating tryptic Tau peptides via chromatography and identifying them based on their unique mass to charge ( m/z ) fragmentation signatures with high specificity and multiplexing capacity (Fig. 4 D). A CSF based PRM study covering 728 individuals across the AD continuum demonstrated AUCs of 0.84–0.98, with p‑T217 offering the highest diagnostic separation [ 30 ]. In a recent BioFINDER-2 head to head comparison, MS-based plasma p‑T217 measurements explained 72% of Aβ-PET variance (vs ≤ 58% for immunoassay-based methods) and aligned more closely with Tau-PET across clinical stages [ 114 ]. However, barriers to widespread MS adoption include high equipment costs, lower throughput, and the lack of globally harmonized calibrators [ 115 ]. All these current approaches face significant bottlenecks. For instance, CSF collection is invasive, high end digital and MS platforms are resource intensive, and most assays are site-specific, overlooking the &gt; 90 post translational Tau modifications known to occur in disease progression [ 30 ]. These constraints have catalyzed the development of next generation diagnostic platforms, including peptide and aptamer-based biosensors, label free photonic devices, and CRISPR based detection. Such platforms promise real time, multiplexed p‑Tau detection at lower cost and complexity, an essential step toward scalable and equitable AD diagnostics. Biosensor technologies for Tau detection Biosensors offer transformative potential for Tau-related diagnostics by combining high sensitivity, rapid response, and miniaturization. Recent innovations have leveraged multiple platforms such as electrochemical, optical, and label-free modalities to detect both total Tau and p‑Tau in body fluids, Fig. 4 A. Electrochemical biosensors Electrochemical biosensors transduce molecular recognition events, such as p‑Tau binding to immobilized antibodies or engineered peptides, into quantifiable electrical signals (e.g., current, voltage, or impedance shifts), (Fig. 4 A, i). These platforms benefit from high surface to volume ratios, label-free operation, and rapid response times, making them particularly attractive for miniaturized, low cost Alzheimer's diagnostics. Their performance can be further enhanced by incorporating nanostructured electrodes (e.g., MWCNTs, graphene hybrids), enzymatic amplification, and microfluidic integration. Several recent platforms have demonstrated sub-picogram to femtogram sensitivity for p-Tau species in clinically relevant fluids: A screen printed carbon electrode decorated with multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs) and platinum nanoparticles achieved detection of p‑Tau181 in serum across 8.6–1100 pg/mL, with a limit of detection (LOD) of 0.24 pg/mL and strong reproducibility, suggesting suitability for point of care use [ 15 ]. An electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) sensor detected plasma p‑Tau181 down to 0.92 fg/mL, with 95% sensitivity and 85% specificity for differentiating Alzheimer's disease patients from controls (AUC = 0.94) [ 116 ]. A 2024 dual target system employed CNT-metal graphene hybrid nano interdigitated electrodes and enzymatic signal amplification to detect p‑Tau181 simultaneously (LOD: 0.08 pg/mL) and p‑Tau217 (LOD: 0.16 pg/mL) in plasma with excellent analytical performance [ 21 ]. Electrochemical biosensors have made remarkable progress toward ultrasensitive, minimally invasive detection of p-Tau biomarkers in clinically relevant fluids. The ability to achieve sub-picogram or even femtogram detection limits, coupled with high specificity, positions these devices as strong contenders for early Alzheimer's disease screening and monitoring. The use of nanostructured materials (e.g., CNT-metal hybrids, Pt nanoparticles) consistently enhances electron transfer efficiency and probe immobilization density, translating into improved analytical performance. However, several translational challenges remain. Matrix effects from complex fluids such as plasma can still compromise long-term stability and reproducibility outside controlled laboratory conditions. Furthermore, while proof-of-concept studies frequently report impressive sensitivity, few have undergone large-scale clinical validation to confirm performance across diverse populations and disease stages. Integration with portable, user-friendly readout systems and microfluidics will be critical for point-of-care deployment. Optical &amp; plasmonic biosensors Optical and plasmonic biosensors leverage changes in light properties, such as refractive index, polarization, or scattering, induced by specific biomolecular interactions (Fig. 4 A, ii). These platforms offer exceptional sensitivity, often down to attomolar concentrations, and are inherently suited for label free, real time analysis. Their versatility enables integration with microfluidics, imaging systems, and even machine-learning algorithms, expanding their potential for point of care and stratified diagnostics in Alzheimer's disease. A notable example is the plasmonic asymmetric nanobridge (PAN) sensor, which achieved attomolar sensitivity for detecting multiple p‑Tau species in plasma. When coupled with supervised machine learning, the platform accurately classified individuals into healthy, mild cognitive impairment (MCI), and Alzheimer's disease groups with 94.5% diagnostic accuracy, highlighting its potential for early, multi-marker-based disease staging [ 89 ]. Another innovative approach uses liquid crystal-based aptamer sensors, where Tau–aptamer binding induces optical birefringence changes in the aligned liquid crystal matrix. This simple, label-free format achieved visual detection of total Tau at 11–20 pg/mL in serum and plasma, offering an accessible platform for resource-limited or bedside diagnostics [ 117 ]. In the category of label free optical sensors, a microfluidic optical fiber biosensor functionalized with anti-Tau441 antibodies on nanopatterned polymer films enabled quantitative detection of Tau in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF)-like matrices across a dynamic range of 1 ng/mL to 10 μg/mL. The system demonstrates real-time signal acquisition with high stability and reproducibility, and is compatible with miniaturized analytical workflows [ 118 ]. Many optical/plasmonic biosensor studies report ultra-low detection limits in buffer conditions; however, when applied to minimally processed human plasma the sensitivity often worsens by one to two orders of magnitude due to nonspecific adsorption and formation of protein corona. To mitigate matrix effects, studies increasingly apply antifouling surface chemistries, blocking agents, and matrix-matched calibration [ 91 , 119 ]. Additionally, in real plasma or serum runs, gradual signal drift and baseline instability are also common, mainly caused by surface fouling and refractive index fluctuations. For that, on-chip reference channels, per run signal re-zeroing and short regeneration steps between assays are recommended for practical stabilization. Depending on sensor design and coating durability, recalibration is typically required every run or at least daily [ 120 ]. Optical alignment, small temperature shifts (sub- ℃ to few ℃) and mechanical vibration measurably change refractive-index readouts; best practice papers recommend temperature control (± 0.5 ℃ or better), differential/dummy channels and mechanical isolation for translation to native matrices [ 121 , 122 ]. Together, translating these promising platforms into clinical workflows requires maintaining robust optical alignment in portable formats, minimizing interference from complex biological matrices, and conducting large-scale validation studies to confirm diagnostic accuracy. Future progress will likely focus on multiplexing capabilities for simultaneous detection of multiple p-Tau epitopes, miniaturization of optical setups, and seamless integration with machine-learning algorithms for automated, high-accuracy diagnostics. Biosensors for intracellular Tau Intracellular biosensor offers a complementary approach to extracellular detection platforms, enabling real time monitoring of tau oligomerization and conformational dynamics within live cells, critical for mechanistic understanding and high-throughput drug discovery (Fig. 5 B). (2023) introduced a NanoBiT-based Tau biosensor system using full length and P301L mutant Tau (2N4R) in living HEK293 cells. By fusing Tau with NanoLuc fragments (LgBiT and SmBiT), the system dynamically reports both intramolecular conformational changes and intermolecular Tau self-association via real time luminescence (Fig. 5 B, i). The assay demonstrates high sensitivity and compatibility with high throughput screening, accurately detecting kinase inhibitor effects on Tau assembly [ 123 , 124 ]. Split NanoLuc complementation has also been applied to monitor Tau dynamics in live-cell models. NanoLuc fragments fused to N- and C-terminal domains of Tau (2N4R) reconstitute enzymatic activity upon phosphorylation-induced oligomerization, providing a sensitive and reversible readout of tau aggregation [ 125 ]. FRET and bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) Earlier fluorescent techniques, including FRET and fluoroprotein fragment complementation have been used to monitor Tau interactions in living cells, though with limitations in sensitivity and throughput. FRET sensors with fluorescent protein tagged Tau (e.g., CFP/YFP) quantify Tau oligomerization through lifetime or intensity-based measurements and are compatible with flow cytometry and screening platforms (Fig. 5 B, ii). BiFC similarly reports Tau–Tau interactions via reconstitution of split-GFP [ 126 ]. These genetically encoded biosensors offer dynamic, spatially resolved insights into tau aggregation, surpassing biochemical assays in throughput and live cell relevance. By capturing both conformational and self-association events within cells, they are powerful tools for evaluating therapeutic interventions, tracking Tau pathology, and mechanistically interrogating intracellular processes in Alzheimer's disease [ 127 ]. Affinity reagents for tau recognition Sensitive and selective detection of Tau, particularly its hyperphosphorylated and aggregation-prone species, depends critically on the development of robust affinity reagents. These molecular recognition elements not only define biosensor specificity but also influence signal fidelity, stability, and translatability across platforms. Emerging strategies now span antibodies, aptamers, and engineered peptides, each offering distinct advantages in stability, manufacturability, and integration with sensor technologies, summarized in comparison in Table 4 . Their compact size, thermal stability, and consistent batch production make them highly promising for biosensor miniaturization and multiplexing across electrochemical, optical, and label free platforms [ 129 ]. Aptamers Single stranded nucleic acids that fold into specific three-dimensional structure have emerged as flexible alternatives to antibodies. DNA aptamers targeting p‑Tau epitopes such as p‑Tau231 have shown binding affinities between 5–68 nM and can modulate tau aggregation in vitro [ 130 ]. When coupled with electrochemical transduction, aptamer-based biosensors have achieved remarkable sensitivity: for example, a gold nanoparticle-enhanced electrochemical aptasensor for p‑Tau231 reported a detection limit of 2.31 pg/mL in serum [ 19 ]. Hybrid systems that combine aptamers with antibodies in sandwich configurations have also demonstrated picomolar detection thresholds, such as a 0.42 pM sensitivity for tau-381, underscoring the benefit of integrating complementary recognition modalities [ 131 ]. Peptide-based affinity reagents Short peptides represent a promising and underexploited class of tau recognition elements. Compared to antibodies and aptamers, peptides are easier to synthesize, chemically stable, and amenable to rational or computational design. Several D-amino acid peptides, developed via mirror-image phage display, have demonstrated selective binding to Tau's aggregation prone hexapeptide motifs (e.g., PHF6 and PHF6*), where they inhibit fibrillization and reduce cellular toxicity [ 132 ]. More recently, designed peptides such as ISAD1 and W-MINK have shown therapeutic efficacy in shifting tau aggregation toward inert high molecular weight species, thereby protecting against tau-induced neurotoxicity in cell and animal models [ 133 ]. Beyond therapeutic applications, these peptides hold strong potential as recognition elements in biosensors, where their small size and robust performance can improve probe stability and response times. Despite promising preclinical results, challenges remain in translating peptide-based tau binders to clinical use. In vivo stability, blood–brain barrier penetration, and avoidance of off-target interactions are critical considerations that require systematic optimization. Moreover, while several peptides have shown the ability to modulate tau aggregation or toxicity, few have progressed to advanced preclinical stages, and even fewer have been evaluated in combination with diagnostic platforms. A growing body of evidence supports the integration of peptide-based tau binders not only in therapeutic strategies but also in next-generation biosensors, potentially enabling highly specific, stable, and scalable tools for both treatment and early diagnosis of Alzheimer's disease. Implications for biosensor design The selection of affinity reagents plays a pivotal role in shaping biosensor architecture, sensitivity, and application scope. Monoclonal antibodies and antibody-derived scaffolds remain the benchmark for diagnostic specificity, particularly in validated immunoassay formats such as ELISA and SIMOA. Their robust performance continues to anchor many clinical grade platforms for tau detection, especially where regulatory approval and reproducibility are critical. Aptamers, with their tunable chemistry, batch-to-batch consistency, and compatibility with label free and electrochemical transduction, have demonstrated detection of p‑Tau in complex fluids at concentrations ranging from femtograms to picograms per milliliter. Their small size and low immunogenicity also make them ideal candidates for integration into microfluidic, wearable, or vivo sensing formats. Synthetic peptides, particularly those composed of D-amino acids or designed via computational methods, emerging as versatile tools for both recognition and functional interrogation of pathological Tau. Unlike antibodies or aptamers, which often target linear or conformational epitopes, peptides can be tailored to interact with aggregation prone motifs (PHF6, PHF6*) or allosteric sites, thus enabling the development of biosensors that both report on and modulate tau oligomerization dynamics. This opens the door to next-generation biosensors that function not only as diagnostic tools, but also as "sensor-therapeutic" hybrids capable of detecting and neutralizing toxic species in real time. Future biosensors may benefit from hybrid recognition designs. For example, combining aptamers with peptides to enable dual-site engagement, or fusing synthetic binders with signal generating domains (cpFPs, FRET pairs, or NanoBiT reporters) for real time intracellular tracking. Additionally, the use of genetically encodable peptide scaffolds, such as those in circularly permuted fluorescent protein (cpFP)-based biosensors, may provide compact, modular, and cell compatible solutions for live cell and high throughput applications. Conclusions and future perspectives P‑Tau epitopes remain among the most clinically informative biomarkers for the early detection and staging of Alzheimer's disease (AD). While ultrasensitive immunoassays have established analytical benchmarks for p‑Tau quantification, their centralization, cost, and infrastructural requirements limit accessibility, particularly in low resource settings or longitudinal monitoring. In response, biosensor technologies have rapidly evolved, aiming to offer portable, multiplexed, and cost effective platforms that maintain clinical grade performance. Recent advances in biosensor engineering, including electro-chemiluminescence (ECL), field effect transistor (FET) designs, and photonic nanostructures, have significantly expanded the analytical capabilities of tau diagnostics. Several platforms now demonstrate detection limits in the attomolar range, particularly when applied in controlled buffer systems. Furthermore, nanoplasmonic asymmetric nanobridge (PAN) sensors have demonstrated multiplexed detection of p-Tau epitopes in patient plasma with ~ 94% diagnostic accuracy, showing promise for translating tau diagnostics toward point of care (POC) and home-use formats with laboratory grade sensitivity [ 20 , 89 , 134 , 135 ]. Despite these milestones, several challenges must be addressed to realize the clinical potential of next-generation tau biosensors fully. First, the field remains heavily reliant on antibodies and aptamers for target recognition. While these bioreceptors offer high affinity and specificity, they are prone to degradation, have limited shelf life, and are difficult to use in intracellular or genetically encoded applications. Synthetic peptides, especially D-peptides and computationally designed sequences, represent a promising alternative, with advantages in thermal stability, low immunogenicity, and structural tunability. Although peptide-based biosensors for p‑Tau have not yet been realized, their feasibility is supported by successful applications in other domains. For instance, peptide-modified FETs have been used to detect kinase activity with high sensitivity [ 135 – 137 ], and phage display-derived peptides have been employed in SPR biosensors for viral protein recognition [ 138 , 139 ]. These precedents suggest strong potential for adapting peptide bioreceptors to Tau and other neurodegenerative biomarkers. Second, translating biosensor technologies to the clinic will require rigorous standardization and validation. Lessons from the development of p‑Tau immunoassays, including multi-center benchmarking and the use of certified reference materials, should inform biosensor evaluation protocols to ensure reproducibility, robustness, and regulatory compliance. Third, true multiplexing and usability must be prioritized. Devices that can simultaneously detect several tau epitopes or co-biomarkers (Aβ42, NFL, GFAP) will provide more nuanced staging and mechanistic insight. Future biosensor platforms should be designed for ease of use, minimal sample handling, and compatibility with decentralized diagnostic workflows. Finally, intracellular and in vivo sensing remains a frontier for tau research. FRET-based and NanoBiT split luciferase reporters have already visualized tau–tau interactions, aggregation kinetics, and secretion dynamics in living cells and mouse neurons [ 123 , 125 ]. By contrast, no fully validated cpFP-based reporter for Tau has yet been published; adapting bright cpFP scaffolds with improved targeting and photophysics, therefore, represents a high-impact development path. The next wave of biosensors for Alzheimer's disease will likely emerge from the integration of peptide-based recognition elements, advanced nanomaterials, and signal amplification strategies tailored for real world deployment. While still in early stages for p‑Tau detection, peptide-enabled biosensors represent a compelling path forward due to their scalability, design flexibility, and compatibility with both in vitro and genetically encoded platforms. As interdisciplinary collaborations bridge gaps between molecular design, materials science, and translational neuroscience, compact, clinically validated, and multiplexed biosensors may soon bring early and accessible Alzheimer's diagnostics into everyday healthcare.
