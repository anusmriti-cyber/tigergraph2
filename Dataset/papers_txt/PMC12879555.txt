A goal of many clinical trials for Alzheimer℉s disease is to develop disease-modifying therapies for the pre-dementia period of the Alzheimer℉s disease process. However, there are no clinical criteria for characterizing subjects in this range. The construct of amnestic mild cognitive impairment has been extensively investigated and may provide useful clinical criteria for this clinical stage. It is proposed that the clinical criteria for mild cognitive impairment be enriched with imaging and other biomarkers to improve the specificity of the outcome, and these criteria might suffice for pre-dementia clinical trials. The assumption underlying this position pertains to early intervention to try to prevent subsequent damage to the brain. For the past decade or more, the construct of mild cognitive impairment (MCI), has been proposed as a clinical point in the spectrum of AD and other dementias representing the pre-dementia state of cognitive impairment. These individuals have a cognitive impairment, usually a memory disorder, beyond what would be expected for age, but not of sufficient severity to represent clinical dementia ( 1 ). This construct has been investigated extensively worldwide with thousands of publications ranging from the epidemiology of MCI to the basic mechanism of disease underlying the clinical symptoms ( 2 ). While there has been a great deal of controversy concerning the implementation of the criteria, there is also a coalescence of data documenting its incidence, prevalence, and propensity for developing dementia. With respect to AD, amnestic MCI (aMCI) of a degenerative etiology has been characterized as the prodromal condition. Amnestic MCI refers to individuals with a memory impairment beyond aging, relative preservation of general cognitive function and functional activities, and the individuals do not meet standard criteria for dementia ( 3 ). In general, individuals meeting these criteria progress to clinical AD at a rate of 10-15% per year when the individuals have been identified in a clinical setting such as a specialty clinic or research center and 7-10% per year when the individuals have been identified in an epidemiological setting ( 4 ). Both of these ranges of rates exceed the baseline rate of AD incidence in the general population of 1-2% per year. As such, aMCI of a degenerative etiology is highly likely to evolve to AD but does not necessarily do so in all cases ( 5 ). An effort to describe this pre-dementia AD has been put forth by Dubois and colleagues ( 6 ). This proposal suggests that a cutoff score on a single episodic memory test would constitute the clinical criteria for pre-dementia AD. The authors then suggest that additional neuroimaging and biomarkers will enhance the specificity of the outcome. Therefore, AD could be identified at a much earlier state in the disease process and offer the opportunity for intervention. It appears as if there were two similar but slightly different approaches to the identification of pre-dementia AD, and the proposal of aMCI of a degenerative etiology and the clinical performance on an episodic memory test. A recent study compared these two approaches with respect to their relative efficiency at predicting dementia ( 7 ). This study involved 1,261 non-demented subjects who were enrolled in a longitudinal study of aging ( 8 ). Subjects were followed from 1 to 18 years, and all of the participants had their memory performance assessed with the Free and Cued Selective Reminding Test as well as being diagnosed as either cognitively normal or meeting MCI criteria at the outset. Clinical MCI status was associated with an increased risk of dementia (HR = 10.15, 95% CI: 7.59-13.57, p &lt; 0.001). Those subjects with an episodic memory score less than the recommended cutoff also had an increased risk of developing dementia (HR = 9.10, CI: 6.72-12.33, p &lt; 0.001). When both criteria were included in the same model, the MCI clinical diagnosis outperformed the episodic memory cutoff score (p &lt; 0.001). Pre-dementia AD can be identified clinically by invoking aMCI criteria of a degenerative etiology. The specificity of the outcome can then be enhanced by adding neuroimaging measures such as structural MI measurements of the medial temporal lobe, functional imaging such as found on FDG PET, biochemical markers such as cerebrospinal fluid tau or A 1-42 and molecular imaging measures such as amyloid PET. Therefore, it is proposed that aMCI be the initial clinical entity to characterize individuals at increased risk of developing dementia on the AD spectrum and the subsequent use of neuroimaging and biomarkers be used to enhance the specificity of the outcomes.
