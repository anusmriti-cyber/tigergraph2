Abstract Alzheimer's disease (AD) is a complex neurodegenerative disorder characterized by progressive cognitive decline. Although amyloid‐β and tau pathologies remain central to our understanding of AD, growing evidence suggests that disrupted lipid metabolism and impaired bioenergetics are closely linked to these hallmark features. Genetic, lipidomic and functional studies point to alterations in cholesterol, phospholipids and polyunsaturated fatty acids, which can influence mitochondrial function, organelle communication and glial responses. These processes are further modulated by apolipoprotein E (APOE) genotype, sex differences and systemic metabolic states such as obesity and diabetes, contributing to neuroinflammation and cognitive decline. Although findings are sometimes conflicting, an emerging theme is that lipid and energy metabolisms are central to how genetic and environmental risk factors shape AD pathogenesis. This integrated perspective highlights lipid and bioenergetic pathways as promising therapeutic targets, where metabolic modulators, lipid‐directed interventions and lifestyle strategies may complement amyloid‐based therapies and offer opportunities for precision approaches, particularly in women and APOE ε4 carriers. The disease affects memory, cognition and behaviour, resulting in a large social and economic burden. Dementia imposes healthcare costs of over €300 billion in the European Union, with similar figures in the United States. These costs are expected to rise sharply due to ageing populations and increasing care demands [ 1 , 2 ]. AD is defined by several pathological hallmarks, including the build‐up of amyloid β‐peptide (Aβ) in the brain's parenchyma and blood vessels, the formation of hyper‐phosphorylated tau protein aggregates within neurons, persistent neuroinflammation, and progressive synaptic and neuronal loss. The risk of AD is estimated to be 60%–80% attributable to heritable (genetic) factors [ 3 ]. Early onset familial forms of AD (FAD) caused by mutations in the amyloid precursor protein ( APP ), presenilin 1 ( PSEN1 ) and 2 ( PSEN2 ) genes are rare. Genetic polymorphisms such as the apolipoprotein E ( APOE ) ε4 allele significantly increase the risk of sporadic late‐onset AD (LOAD). In addition to genetic contributors, biological (e.g., older age and sex) and environmental (e.g., lifestyle) factors also play a role in the onset and progression of the disease [ 4 ]. Understanding the mechanisms that drive AD onset and progression remains a major challenge, but recent large‐scale omics studies highlight the pivotal role of lipid metabolism in its pathogenesis [ 5 ]. Lipids are crucial for brain cellular function, signalling and physiology, including, but not limited to, cellular membrane composition, integrity and dynamics, synaptic activity and energy production. Mitochondria—vital for energy production—are highly affected by lipid content, which influences their structure, dynamics and bioenergetics. This review will explore lipid metabolism and its crosstalk with mitochondrial energy regulation in the context of AD pathogenesis, while also considering systemic metabolic interactions and lifestyle factors. These insights offer promising directions for understanding AD mechanisms and progression and identifying therapeutic targets. From glucose to lipids: a 50‐year journey into metabolic alterations in AD Metabolic deficits are among the earliest and most consistent features of AD. The brain's high energy demand makes neurons particularly vulnerable to even subtle changes in fuel utilization, and disturbances in glucose and lipid metabolism are increasingly recognized as contributors to disease risk and progression. Early evidence came from 2‐[ 18 F]fluoro‐2‐deoxy‐ d ‐glucose position emission tomography (FDG‐PET) studies, which revealed hypometabolism in temporoparietal and posterior cingulate cortices, correlating with cognitive decline and Aβ deposition [ 6 ], and similar changes were observed in APOE ε4 carriers and in patients with mild cognitive impairment (MCI, i.e., prodromal AD) who later converted to AD [ 6 , 7 ]. At the molecular level, mitochondria show reduced activity of respiratory complexes—particularly complex IV—and tricarboxylic acid cycle enzymes and are directly affected by Aβ and tau interactions with the translocase machinery [ 8 ]. Several independent studies in prodromal AD brains and in different AD mouse models (PS1‐A246E mutant mice and APP knock‐in) [ 9 , 10 , 11 ] suggest a phase of metabolic reprogramming preceding overt plaque deposition, with neuronal hypermetabolism and glial shifts towards glycolysis and lactate production, possibly as compensatory responses [ 12 ]. Proteomic and metabolomic data support this view, showing upregulation of glycolytic enzymes and increased carbohydrate metabolism in AD subtypes with hyperplasticity phenotypes [ 13 , 14 ]. When glucose utilization becomes insufficient, mitochondria can metabolize fatty acids (FAs) and ketone bodies. However, evidence is conflicting: Some studies report enhanced FA β‐oxidation in astrocytes exposed to Aβ, whereas others demonstrate downregulation of FA oxidation enzymes and inefficient clearance of neuronal FAs by astrocytes, leading to oxidative stress and lipotoxicity [ 15 , 16 ]. Lipidomic analyses also show distinct FA profiles in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and plasma, with specific FAs correlating to conversion from MCI to AD [ 17 ]. Overall, although alternative fuels may temporarily support energy demands, reliance on lipids becomes maladaptive over time and contributes to the collapse of mitochondrial energy production in AD. Genetic basis for the contribution of lipid and metabolic imbalance to AD pathology Increasing genetic evidence suggests a role for lipid metabolism dysregulation in the pathogenesis of LOAD. Large‐scale genome‐wide association studies (GWAS) of common genetic variants have currently identified 81 loci associated with AD and consistently identified an enrichment in lipid transport, inflammation and metabolism‐related pathways [ 18 , 19 ]. In addition to the well‐known main genetic risk factor for LOAD, APOE ε4, variants in the CLU locus (that encodes clusterin or apolipoprotein J) are associated with an increased risk of AD [ 20 ]. A recent GWAS study of different neuropathology endophenotypes identified associations for the broader APOE region with multiple AD endophenotypes and identified a new association for the APOC2 locus with cerebral amyloid angiopathy [ 21 ]. Recent exome sequencing studies, which can identify more rare variants, found damaging polymorphisms in ABCA1 and ATP8B4 [ 22 ]. These two genes code for cholesterol and phospholipid efflux mediators that affect ApoE lipidation. Lipidation is essential for ApoE's structural conformation and function, enabling its interaction with Aβ peptides and promoting their clearance from the brain [ 23 , 24 ]. Alterations in lipoprotein composition and function related to these variants may, therefore, exacerbate Aβ build‐up and AD pathogenesis. Besides lipid transport and ApoE function, variants in the ABCA7 [ 25 ] locus—a modulator of mitochondrial metabolism of phospholipids [ 26 ]—suggest a role for metabolic alterations in AD pathogenesis. This is further supported by a recent study that integrated GWAS with human brain proteomics data, reporting a causal association with AD for LACTB [ 27 ]. LACTB encodes a mitochondrial protease involved in lipid metabolism through its modulatory effect on several enzymes, such as phosphatidyl serine decarboxylase [ 28 ] and phospholipase A2 [ 29 ]. This study also identified an association with EPHX2 , which is involved in the degradation of arachidonic acid metabolites generated by the cytochrome p450 system (CYP). These bioactive metabolites can regulate cerebral blood flow by promoting vasodilation and modulating neuroinflammation. Polymorphisms that affect the activity of EPHX2 have been associated with oxygen‐glucose deprivation‐induced neuronal cell death [ 30 ]. A recent GWAS identified a variant in the CYP1B1‐RMND2 locus that is associated with a tau deposition endophenotype based on cortical tau PET burden [ 31 ]. CYP1B1 —highly expressed in excitatory neurons—codes for a member of the CYP enzyme family. Notably, several CYP genes—such as CYP17 , CYP19 and CYP46A1 —have been associated with AD pathology [ 32 , 33 ]. CYP family members participate in the metabolism of lipids, steroids and xenobiotics and contribute to redox homeostasis [ 34 ], which plays an essential role in mitochondrial biology and vice versa. Alterations in redox homeostasis that lead to oxidative stress will contribute to tau pathology and neuroinflammation. TOMM40 —which encodes a protein component of the mitochondrial transmembrane channel essential for protein import into mitochondria—is part of the extended APOE locus that includes regulatory elements and is in strong linkage disequilibrium with APOE [ 35 , 36 ]. These data highlight a role for mitochondrial dysfunction in AD pathology and indicate a complex interplay between lipid metabolism, oxidative stress and neurodegeneration. Lipid metabolism in AD As early as 1906, Alois Alzheimer noted, along with the presence of Aβ plaques and tau neurofibrillary tangles, lipid inclusions in the brains of patients with AD, especially in glial cells [ 37 ]. Since then, abundant clinical and preclinical evidence has underscored lipid dysregulation as a possible driver of AD risk, pathogenesis and progression. The lipid composition of the brain is unique, with a large proportion of cholesterol, sphingolipids and phospholipids concentrated in myelin sheaths, neuronal membranes and microdomains known as lipid raft‐platforms for cell signalling, neuronal communication and Aβ production [ 38 , 39 ]. Lipidomics have consistently shown changes in most lipid classes during AD progression, with early alterations occurring in patients and mouse models [ 40 , 41 ]. Numerous sphingolipids and their metabolites, including ceramides, are elevated in the CSF and brains of patients with AD, which is believed to alter lipid raft composition and promote the amyloidogenic pathway of Aβ production [ 42 ]. Conversely, AD brains exhibit reduced levels of phospholipids, including phosphatidylcholines and phosphatidylserines, which compromise membrane fluidity and synaptic vesicle function [ 43 , 44 ]. Decreases in phospholipids are also detected in the plasma of cognitively healthy individuals who later develop AD, suggesting the presence of early alterations in neural cell membranes [ 44 ]. Accumulation of Aβ may directly disrupt the membrane bilayer integrity by interacting with phospholipids, and cells treated with Aβ show alterations in their lipidome [ 45 ]. A recent study employing spatial neurolipidomics showed how the lipid microenvironment in the proximity of amyloid plaques is greatly altered in human post‐mortem AD brains [ 46 ]. Whether lipidome alterations contribute to AD pathogenesis or are a consequence of the pathological processes is not yet fully understood. Polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), omega‐3 and ‐6 FAs, are essential components of phospholipids. The omega FAs, particularly docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), are vital for brain development, and their deficiency can impair cerebral functions and perturb the composition of cellular membranes in neurons, astrocytes and oligodendrocytes, and of myelin, synaptosomes and mitochondria [ 47 ]. PUFAs have been shown to support mitochondrial biogenesis and modulate energy metabolism‐related gene expression [ 48 ]. Recent studies showed reduced levels of PUFA derivatives in the AD brain, that is, small bioactive lipid mediators that are of importance for regaining homeostasis after inflammation (see ‘ Lipid mediators: key players in neuroinflammation and Its Resolution ’ section) [ 45 , 49 , 50 ]. The brain contains 25% of the body's total cholesterol, and sterol metabolism—including cholesterol—contributes to AD pathology [ 65 ]. As cholesterol cannot cross the blood–brain barrier (BBB), most cholesterol is synthesized de novo in the brain, mainly by astrocytes [ 65 ]. Cholesterol is then delivered through ApoE transporters to neurons, where it is essential for synapse formation and neuronal activity [ 66 ]. Cholesterol is also integral to myelin structure and function. Cholesterol synthesis, transport and efflux are significantly dysregulated in AD [ 67 , 68 , 69 ]. This dysregulation is further exacerbated by the accumulation of Aβ plaques, which impairs cholesterol transport and processing [ 70 ]. Cholesterol metabolites, including oxysterols, accumulate, whereas the expression of CYP46A1, the neuronal enzyme responsible for eliminating excess cholesterol and its product, 24(S)‐hydroxycholesterol, decrease. This indicates dysregulation of cholesterol homeostasis due to reduced catabolism and efflux [ 71 , 72 , 73 ]. Not only is CYP46A1 impaired during AD, but also the other complementary pathway for excess cholesterol elimination, which involves cholesterol esterification to cholesteryl esters and their storage in lipid droplets (LDs) [ 74 , 75 ]. LDs consist of a phospholipid monolayer surrounding a core of neutral lipids and function as storage stations, facilitating the degradation of their contents via mitochondria (and peroxisomes), as an alternative energy source [ 76 , 77 ]. Notably, LDs and cholesteryl esters accumulate in AD [ 74 , 75 ]. Altogether, these factors lead to a critical impairment of the glia‐neuron coupling, contributing to AD pathogenic processes such as Aβ overproduction, phospho‐tau accumulation and neuroinflammation [ 78 ]. Cell‐type‐specific contributions to brain lipid metabolism in AD Most lipidomic studies rely on bulk brain tissue analysis, which masks cell‐type‐specific differences, whereas lipidomic profiles appear to differ substantially across brain cell types [ 80 ]. A recent study demonstrated that human induced pluripotent stem cell‐derived neurons, astrocytes and microglia all exhibit distinct lipid profiles: Neurons have high levels of phosphatidylcholine and phosphatidylethanolamine, microglia are enriched in sphingomyelins and phosphatidylserines, and astrocytes have higher levels of cholesterol esters and LDs, reflecting their main role as cholesterol suppliers [ 81 ]. Astrocytes are the brain cell type with the highest expression of the AD risk genes APOE and CLU [ 82 ]. They are particularly sensitive to alterations in lipid metabolism and rely on it to maintain central nervous system homeostasis, provide metabolic support to neurons and regulate synaptic activity [ 83 , 84 ]. Astrocyte‐derived lipids are critical for myelination and myelin maintenance (lipid synthesis by oligodendrocytes is heavily supplemented by astrocytes) [ 85 ]. A recent in vitro study demonstrated that levels of free and esterified cholesterol change depending on astrocyte reactivity to pro‐inflammatory cytokines. Reactive astrocytes store fewer lipids, have lower levels of cholesteryl esters and triglycerides and exhibit higher levels of interferon‐dependent markers, suggesting that cholesterol metabolism directly contributes to astrocyte reactivity and phenotypes [ 81 ]. Mitochondrial FA metabolism is also central to astrocytes and, when dysfunctional, can trigger neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration [ 86 ]. Moreover, LDs secreted by reactive astrocytes can alter the lipid metabolism of surrounding cells. They can stimulate neuronal FA oxidation and oxidative stress, modulate microglia function through interleukin‐3 signalling and inhibit the biosynthesis of FAs and phospholipids [ 87 , 88 ]. These effects are amplified in astrocytes carrying the APOE ε4 genotype. Analysis of post‐mortem AD brain tissue showed that higher synthesis of choline—a phospholipid precursor—in astrocytes is linked to higher cognitive resilience [ 5 ], consistent with the role of astrocytic lipid metabolism in maintaining neuronal health. Given the central involvement of ApoE in astrocyte lipid handling, several preclinical studies have explored astrocyte‐targeted modulation of APOE as a strategy to restore lipid and inflammatory balance. Adeno‐associated virus‐mediated removals of APOE ε4 in astrocytes have demonstrated the capacity to restore synaptic integrity and tau pathology, whereas antisense oligonucleotides that lower APOE expression alleviated neuroinflammation and lipid dysregulation (Table 1 ). Based on this evidence, therapeutic approaches, including, but not limited to, astrocyte‐specific APOE ‐based therapies, hold potential as a therapeutic strategy to restore metabolic homeostasis in AD. Oligodendrocytes produce the myelin sheath, a lipid‐rich structure that insulates neuronal axons and ensures efficient neurotransmission [ 68 ]. Dysregulation of lipid metabolism in these cells has been linked to white matter abnormalities and cognitive decline in several disease conditions, including AD [ 68 , 89 ]. Alterations in cholesterol metabolism in AD and the presence of APOE ε4 lead to reduced myelination capacity and alter myelin turnover, further contributing to demyelination, white matter atrophy and cognitive decline [ 90 , 91 ]. Magnetic resonance imaging studies showed that white matter injury precedes amyloid plaques and neurofibrillary tangles in human early onset FAD and in human LOAD [ 92 ]. Microglia, the resident immune cells of the brain, are also highly responsive to alterations in lipid metabolism, which influence their transcriptional profile and interactions with neurons [ 93 ]. When exposed to amyloid plaques, they produce ApoE and upregulate its expression, together with CLU [ 94 ]. Microglia also express the AD risk gene TREM2 [ 95 ], which is almost exclusively expressed by microglia and peripheral macrophages in the brain. Interestingly, besides being a lipid sensor that mediates microglial response to amyloid pathology [ 90 ], TREM2 modulates their metabolic capacity to clear myelin debris‐derived cholesterol [ 96 ]. TREM2 loss of function leads to the pathogenic accumulation of lipids within microglia, an event that has been observed in AD brains in the form of LDs [ 77 ]. In the presence of tau pathology in frontotemporal dementia, LD accumulation in microglia leads to a pro‐inflammatory phenotype that exacerbates neuronal damage [ 97 ]. The APOE ε4 genotype promotes LD accumulation and lipid dysregulation in both microglia and astrocytes, driving microglial reactivity with reduced phagocytic capacity and impaired clearance of Aβ. This state is associated with increased proximity to amyloid plaques, increased tau phosphorylation and neurotoxicity [ 77 , 98 ] (Fig. 1 ). Unbiased lipidomic analysis identified diacylglycerol acyltransferase (DGAT2) as a key enzyme for converting free FAs to triacylglycerols, promoting microglial LD formation, and the enzyme is increased in the brain of 5xFAD mice and AD [ 99 ]. Targeting microglial lipid metabolism—whether by modulating LD formation, enhancing lipid clearance or mitigating ApoE4‐associated toxicity—holds promise as a therapeutic strategy. Elevated ATAD3A disrupts mitochondrial function and reduces CYP27A1 transcription, impairing cholesterol metabolism. Mitochondria, mitochondrial contacts and lipid metabolism regulation in AD Mitochondria regulate lipid metabolism while also being influenced by it. Mitochondrial β‐oxidation of FAs produces acetyl‐CoA and reducing equivalents required for ATP synthesis, whereas FAs accumulation leads to mitochondrial damage. In AD, this process becomes dysregulated, correlating with cognitive decline and neurodegeneration [ 15 , 86 , 100 ]. One key enzyme in this pathway is carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1 (CPT1), which facilitates the transport of long‐chain FAs across the outer mitochondrial membrane (OMM). CPT1 activity is downregulated in the MCI to AD continuum and is further affected by mitochondrial fragmentation [ 101 , 102 , 103 ], a hallmark of AD pathology. Although direct links between mitochondrial lipids and dynamics (including fission and fusion, which drive mitochondrial network connectivity) in AD remain scarce, evidence suggests that lipid alterations can exacerbate mitochondrial fragmentation [ 88 ]. Mitochondrial double membranes are highly enriched in lipids such as cardiolipin, phosphatidylcholine and phosphatidylethanolamine, essential for maintaining structural integrity, cristae morphology and fluidity. Cardiolipin—a mitochondria‐exclusive phospholipid—plays a key role in cristae folding due to its ability to promote negative membrane curvature [ 104 , 105 ]. It anchors the GTPase dynamin‐related protein 1 (DRP1) to the OMM and enhances its oligomerization, modulating its role in mitochondria fission [ 106 , 107 ]. Cardiolipin also supports the assembly of respiratory supercomplexes in the electron transport chain, optimizing OxPHOS and preventing electron leakage that drives reactive oxygen species (ROS) production [ 105 ]. In the brain of patients with AD [ 108 ], triple transgenic mice with AD [ 109 ] and ABCA7‐deficient iPSC‐derived organoids [ 26 , 109 ], decreased cardiolipin levels are associated with ATP deficit and oxidative damage. Additionally, cardiolipin is particularly perturbed by the accumulation of tau, which directly binds to cardiolipin‐rich regions of the OMM, causing mitochondrial swelling and triggering apoptotic pathways associated with neurodegeneration [ 110 , 111 ]. To fulfil their function, mitochondria do not work in isolation but build up dynamic and precise interactions with multiple cellular organelles/sub‐domains, including, but not only, LDs and the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) [ 112 , 113 ]. Those interactions and their relevance to AD are discussed in the following section. Lipid droplet‐mitochondria coupling LD formation is tightly associated with the nutrient status and cellular stress. Although neurons have limited capacity for lipid storage, astrocytes serve as a neuronal detox mechanism by sequestering ROS‐induced peroxidized lipids. This process is mediated by apolipoproteins, and the expression of APOE ε4 allele in astrocytes shifts their metabolism towards reduced FA β‐oxidation, leading to lipid accumulation and fragmented dysfunctional mitochondria [ 88 ] (Fig. 1 ). Consequently, astrocytes show compromised metabolic and synaptic support to neurons [ 88 ]. Although mitochondrial fusion is required to distribute and oxidize transferred FAs, mitochondrial fragmentation—observed in human AD astrocytes [ 15 , 114 , 115 ]—reroutes FAs to LDs [ 116 ], suggesting a critical role of mitochondrial shape in this process. Contrarily, the mitochondrial fission factor DRP1 is required for neuron‐glia lipid transfer and consequent glia‐mediated breakdown of peroxidized lipids accumulated during neuronal activity [ 117 ]. A specific mitochondria subpopulation bound to LDs has recently been described, the peridroplet mitochondria [ 118 ], that enables the cell to support lipid synthesis and oxidation simultaneously. The recruitment of mitochondria to LDs promotes the expansion of LD mass, and it is regulated by the LD‐coat protein perilipin 5 and DGAT2, the latter increased in AD [ 99 , 119 ]. Protein tethers keep these membranes together, including perilipin 1:mitofusin 2 association, with the downregulation of the latter reducing cellular FA β‐oxidation capacity [ 120 ] (Fig. 1 ). Although mitofusin 2 is downregulated in AD, and FAD‐linked mutations in PSEN2 favour the binding and sequestering of mitofusin 2 [ 121 , 122 , 123 ], the role of peridroplet mitochondria in AD remains to be understood. Additionally, although mitofusin 2 is a mitochondrial fusion protein, it also operates at other membrane contact sites, raising doubts about whether its fusion activity or tethering role primarily regulates FA breakdown. Mitochondria‐ER contacts: lipid transport and synthesis Contacts between cholesterol‐rich lipid raft‐like domains of ER and the OMM are named mitochondria‐associated membranes (MAM) [ 124 ], whereas the physical contact points, including the two membranes, are here referred to as mitochondria‐ER contacts (MERCs) [ 112 ]. Scaffolding‐ and contact‐regulating proteins constitute MERCs, which are formed within 20–80 nm distance between the OMM and ER membranes and allow the exchange of signalling and metabolic molecules between the two organelles. Key cellular processes, such as calcium homeostasis, lipid metabolism, ER stress response, mitochondrial dynamics and respiration, formation of autophagosomes and inflammasomes, occur or are modulated at these domains [ 125 ]. These processes are all disturbed in AD, strongly indicating that perturbations of mitochondria‐ER coupling might be linked to AD pathogenesis. MERCs upregulation is a consistent feature of both FAD and LOAD [ 121 , 123 , 126 , 127 , 128 , 129 ], with some disease‐linked proteins such as PS2 [ 123 , 126 ] and APP [ 130 ] involved in this process. Cellular lipid metabolism depends on correct mitochondria‐ER coupling. For example, the synthesis of phosphatidylserine and phosphatidylethanolamine requires the cooperation of the two organelles at MERCs [ 131 ]. This pathway is upregulated in both FAD and LOAD samples, suggesting that MAM‐mediated alterations in lipid homeostasis are linked to AD. Indeed, sphingolipid pathways are also altered in AD [ 132 ], and MERCs contribute to the (dys)regulation of sphingolipid metabolism [ 133 ], increasing MAM‐localized ceramide [ 134 ] shown to modulate γ‐secretase‐mediated cleavage to increase Aβ42 production [ 135 ]. The activity of acyl‐CoA cholesterol acyltransferase—which converts cholesterol into cholesterol esters (accumulated in LDs)—is upregulated in presenilin‐mutant cells and in fibroblasts both from patients with FAD and LOAD [ 128 ]. Additionally, APP‐C99 (generated by β‐secretase cleavage of APP and increased in AD) accumulates in MAM, reinforcing cholesterol clustering and increasing acyl‐CoA cholesterol acyltransferase activity and cholesterol esters deposition in LDs, eventually disturbing the lipid composition of cellular membranes [ 130 , 134 ] (Fig. 1 ). Moreover, modulation of MAM affects phospholipid composition of mitochondrial membranes, including cardiolipin content, contributing to the bioenergetic defects characteristic of AD [ 130 ]. The intimate relationship between MERCs and lipid metabolism is underlined by the activity of the oxysterol binding proteins ORP5 and ORP8, the mitochondrial ATPase family AAA‐domain containing protein 3A (ATAD3A) and the γ‐secretase activating protein (GSAP) (Fig. 1 ). ORP5/8 localize at MAM, where they interact with the tether protein tyrosine phosphatase‐interacting protein 51 (PTPIP51) at the OMM to mediate phosphatidylserine transfer and regulate LD biogenesis [ 136 ]. Overexpression of ORP5/8 leads to their redistribution to MAM‐LDs contacts, whereas reduced levels of PTPIP51—found in the temporal cortex in late stages of AD [ 137 ]—and PTPIP51 knockdown (which decreases MERCs [ 138 ]) impair LD formation. These findings underscore that mitochondria‐ER apposition is required for ORP5/8‐dependent LD genesis. Other MERCs regulators link lipid metabolism more directly to AD pathology. ATAD3A inhibits CYP46A1 transcription, impairing neuronal cholesterol turnover, promoting MERCs hyperconnectivity and synaptic loss [ 139 ]. In 5xFAD mice, Cyp46a1 downregulation associates with amyloid burden and cognitive decline, whereas its overexpression reduces raft cholesterol and amyloidogenic APP processing [ 139 ]. Blocking ATAD3A oligomerization suppresses MAM hyperconnectivity, improves spatial learning and memory and decreases amyloid accumulation [ 139 ]. GSAP, a selective modulator of γ‐secretase activity towards APP processing [ 140 ], is also enriched in MAM and upregulated in AD [ 134 ]. GSAP deletion shortens MERCs and changes their lipidomic profile, creating a lipid environment that prevents AD pathogenesis and improves mitochondrial function [ 141 ]. Its expression increases with age and inflammatory stimuli [ 141 ], and a promoter single‐nucleotide polymorphism associated with higher GSAP levels correlates with greater AD risk [ 142 ]. Although causality remains unclear—whether MERCs dysfunction drives lipid imbalance or vice versa—clarifying these reciprocal mechanisms is essential for understanding AD pathology and identifying therapeutic targets. Lipid mediators: key players in neuroinflammation and its resolution AD pathology presents a strong inflammatory component driven by glial cells and sustained by pro‐inflammatory cytokines, which, together with Aβ, form a vicious cycle leading to neuronal death (for a detailed review, see [ 143 ]). This inflammatory response has been linked to the dysregulation of lipid mediators derived from PUFAs—including DHA [ 144 , 145 , 146 ], eicosapentaenoic acid [ 147 ] and arachidonic acid. Although arachidonic acid‐derived lipid mediators exert both pro‐inflammatory (prostaglandins and leukotrienes) and anti‐inflammatory (lipoxins and epoxides) effects, DHA‐ and eicosapentaenoic acid‐derived lipid mediators were named ‘specialized pro‐resolving lipid mediators’ (SPMs) [ 147 , 148 ]. Several enzymes of the cyclooxygenases (COXs), lipoxygenases (LOXs) and CYP families are upregulated in AD brain, driving excess pro‐inflammatory lipid mediators and microglial reactivity [ 149 , 150 ]. PUFAs are also prone to peroxidation, forming reactive aldehydes such as 4‐HNE and acrolein, which rise early in AD and are further exacerbated by ApoE4 [ 151 ]. Impaired resolution of inflammation has been suggested as a contributing mechanism to AD by promoting chronic neuroinflammation and sustained microglial activation. Serhan et al. [ 152 ] showed that this process is orchestrated by SPMs, which are produced ‘on demand’ by an enzymatic machinery based on the activity of COX, LOX and CYP enzymes [ 148 ]. These bioactive lipids reduce chemotaxis, oxidative stress and pro‐inflammatory cytokine release, while promoting anti‐inflammatory signals and debris clearance [ 145 , 152 ]. In AD, however, persistent low‐level inflammation coincides with reduced levels of SPMs such as neuroprotectin D1, maresin 1, resolvins and lipoxin A4 in the brain and CSF [ 50 , 153 , 154 ]. Receptors for several SPMs (ChemR23, BLT1, FPR2) are increased in neurons and glia, possibly reflecting a compensatory response [ 50 , 155 ]. Although increased SPM receptor expression may represent compensation, it also highlights therapeutic potential. Both in vitro and in vivo studies support SPMs’ beneficial effects: In a human microglial in vitro model, maresin 1 reduces Aβ‐induced cytokine release, reverses Aβ‐driven inflammatory gene expression and enhances Aβ phagocytosis. Lipoxin A4 similarly decreases pro‐inflammatory cytokines [ 156 , 157 ]. In mouse models of amyloid pathology, SPM treatment reduces amyloid burden, improves memory, restores synaptic activity and dampens microglial activation [ 158 , 159 , 160 ]. Together, these findings support SPMs as promising candidates to restore resolution of inflammation in AD. Lipid dysregulation and systemic health in AD Vascular and metabolic health Given the importance of brain vasculature in maintaining brain homeostasis and neurovascular coupling in supporting neuronal function, it is not surprising that vascular alterations result in impaired brain function and cognition. Indeed, diseases associated with poor metabolic health and alterations in lipid metabolism—such as obesity, hyperlipidaemia, prediabetes and diabetes—promote chronic systemic inflammation and vascular pathology and are associated with cognitive impairment in both humans [ 161 , 162 ] and rodents [ 163 , 164 ]. In AD, the specific contribution of vascular alterations to disease onset and progression remains to be fully understood. This may be partially due to the fact that over 80% of AD cases also exhibit vascular pathology, making the identification of causal associations and individual contributions very challenging [ 165 , 166 , 167 ]. Additionally, difficulties in isolating cell types from the brain vasculature in single‐cell transcriptomics studies have further hindered our understanding of the contribution of these cells to the disease. Recent studies have overcome this limitation and described widespread gene expression changes in vascular cells in post‐mortem AD brain tissue [ 82 , 168 ]. Interestingly, integration of these data with AD genetics showed that not only are several AD risk variants highly expressed by the human brain vasculature, but also many AD‐associated risk loci are linked to differentially expressed genes in vascular cells in AD [ 168 ]. For instance, APOE is expressed by vascular mural cells, and the expression of the APOE ε4 allele by these cells contributes to alterations in cerebral blood flow and synaptic plasticity‐associated processes [ 169 ]. It is therefore possible that different AD drivers—such as amyloid pathology—together with genetic and non‐genetic AD risk factors—such as ageing—contribute to the vulnerability of the brain vasculature, an effect that vascular risk factors can further accelerate. The mechanisms by which poor metabolic health and consequent lipid dysregulation may contribute to AD are likely multifaceted. High‐fat diets promote hyperlipidaemia and insulin resistance, which can result in either prediabetes or type 2 diabetes (T2D) [ 164 , 170 , 171 ]. These changes are associated with vascular consequences, such as impaired endothelial [ 172 , 173 , 174 , 175 , 176 ] and BBB function [ 177 , 178 , 179 , 180 ], vascular remodelling [ 181 ], impaired neurovascular coupling [ 176 ], neuroinflammation and reduced brain insulin signalling [ 182 ]. Hyperlipidaemia also affects cognition via oxysterols, BBB‐permeable oxidized cholesterol metabolites [ 65 , 183 ] (Fig. 2 ). High‐fat and cholesterol diets affect memory and hippocampal morphology as well as BBB integrity [ 184 , 185 ] and alter lipid profiles and elevate oxysterol levels, especially 27‐hydroxycholesterol (27OH) [ 186 , 187 ], which crosses the BBB and activates brain nuclear receptors [ 188 ]. In rodent models and patients with AD, elevated 27OH induces synaptic dysfunction, oxidative stress, neuroinflammation and impaired glucose metabolism, via modulation of the angiotensin system [ 189 , 190 , 191 , 192 , 193 ]. In line with this, lowering 27OH levels prevents cognitive deficits associated with high‐fat diets in mice [ 194 ]. Fig. 2 Brain body‐interactions and their contribution to Alzheimer's disease (AD). (iv) Muscle and metabolic stress: Frailty and muscle atrophy disrupt systemic energy homeostasis (e.g., increased lactate, decreased nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD) and brain‐derived neurotrophic factor [BDNF]). Beyond therapies directly targeting AD pathology, approaches aimed at mitigating the effects of poor metabolic health and dysregulated lipid metabolism are gaining attention. These include statins to reduce hyperlipidaemia [ 195 ], GLP‐1 agonists for obesity and T2D [ 196 ] and insulin sensitizers such as metformin [ 197 ] (Table 1 ). Drugs targeting oxysterol‐converting enzymes such as CYP27A1 and CYP46A1 have shown promising results [ 55 ], and lowering 27OH levels has been linked to improved cognition in a 2‐year multimodal prevention trial [ 198 ] (Table 1 ). Despite encouraging findings, more robust clinical trials are still needed to determine whether these therapies can slow the progression of dementia. Given the parallel rise in metabolic disorders and AD, clarifying their interplay with vascular function and AD pathology remains critical for future therapeutic strategies [ 196 , 197 ]. Interplay between frailty, body mass index and obesity Muscle and fat are key contributors to systemic metabolism. Frailty is defined as reduced physiological reserve and increased vulnerability to adverse outcomes and mortality. Clinically, it is characterized by the frailty phenotype, which includes unintentional weight loss, exhaustion, weakness, slowness and low physical activity and overlaps with many symptoms observed in patients with AD [ 199 ]. Mounting evidence indicates that frailty can accelerate cognitive decline (Fig. 2 ) and increase vulnerability to neuroinflammation and metabolic dysregulation [ 200 , 201 ]. AD and frailty share genetic risk loci ( CRHR1 , FERMT2 and GRK4 ) [ 202 ] and converge on pathways involving inflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction, proteostasis dysregulation (impaired Aβ clearance in AD; broader proteostasis deficits in frailty) and shared additional risk factors such as age and T2D with vascular damage [ 8 , 203 , 204 ]. Body mass index (BMI) and obesity are important modifiers of frailty and AD risk [ 205 , 206 ] (Fig. 2 ). The direct relationship between BMI and AD risk is complex and appears to be age‐dependent: Midlife obesity consistently increases the likelihood of dementia, whereas low BMI in late life is also associated with higher dementia risk, likely reflecting preclinical weight loss and frailty [ 207 , 208 ]. This ‘obesity paradox’ illustrates the complexity of BMI effects, with abdominal obesity specifically linked to increased AD risk [ 206 ], highlighting the importance of fat distribution, sedentary lifestyle and exercise in disease risk. Mechanistic studies provide possible explanations: Obesity promotes insulin resistance, leading to hyperinsulinemia and reduced Aβ clearance as insulin and Aβ compete for their degradation by insulin‐degrading enzyme; impairs leptin signalling and tau pathology; elevates circulating lipids, including elevated free FAs that often result in dyslipidaemia (high LDL and low HDL in the blood result in worse cognitive performance and increased AD severity) [ 209 , 210 , 211 ]. Altered lipid metabolism, including cholesterol and triglycerides, influences APP processing and Aβ deposition [ 210 , 211 , 212 ]. Together, human and experimental evidence highlight BMI and obesity as important risk modifiers, with shared metabolic pathways that indicate a potential therapeutic strategy for managing the disease. Sarcopenia is the age‐related loss of muscle mass and function and has been increasingly linked to AD [ 213 ] through several biological mechanisms, including chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, insulin resistance and hormone dysregulation (Fig. 2 ) [ 214 , 215 ]. Decreased myokine secretion released during contraction and physical inactivity further accelerates muscle atrophy and negatively impacts brain health by limiting neurogenesis and synaptic plasticity partially through key mediators such as lactate, nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide and brain‐derived neurotrophic factor [ 216 ]. Lifestyle interventions targeting these pathways are promising: Exercise and caloric restriction provide neuroprotection in animal models of neurodegeneration [ 217 ], whereas the FINGER (Finnish Geriatric Intervention Study to Prevent Cognitive Impairment and Disability) trial showed that combined strategies of diet, exercise, cognitive training and vascular risk monitoring can help preserve cognition in at‐risk elderly individuals [ 218 ]. The contribution of systemic estrogen levels to AD risk AD is more prevalent in women than in men, and once diagnosed, women display faster disease progression and higher tau pathology [ 219 ]. Furthermore, women heterozygous for APOE ε4 have a higher risk of AD than men [ 220 ]. This suggests that sex‐specific factors (other than age) interact with AD risk. In addition to sex chromosome‐related effects, fluctuations in sex hormones across reproductive phases of a woman's life may contribute to AD: Loss of estrogens at menopause associates with metabolic changes and increased risk for insulin resistance and metabolic syndrome (obesity, hypertension, dyslipidaemia), which in turn may contribute to AD [ 221 ] (Fig. 2 ). In female models of amyloidosis and VCID (vascular contributions to cognitive impairment and dementia) fed on a high‐fat diet, metabolic impairment (obesity and prediabetes) is more severe and accompanied by broad cognitive impairments than in male mice [ 222 , 223 ]. Consistently in humans, diabetes confers a higher VCID risk in women compared to men [ 224 ], and prediabetes links to early onset of AD in women, but not in men [ 225 ]. Decreased estrogen levels can also directly affect brain health and may contribute to an increased risk of developing AD. Estrogens have neuroprotective properties, including antioxidant effects, and can regulate mitochondrial function through the PGC‐1 (peroxisome proliferator‐activated receptor γ coactivator 1) family of mitochondrial biogenesis regulators. Because mitochondrial dysfunction contributes to bioenergetic deficits and oxidative stress—hallmarks of AD—it has been proposed that menopausal loss of estrogens could exacerbate disease progression by such direct actions on mitochondria [ 226 ]. Concordantly, ovariectomy promotes mitochondrial fragmentation in 3xTg AD mouse brains [ 227 ]. In contrast, hormonal therapy during menopausal transition could protect against such vulnerability. However, observational and clinical trial data remain inconclusive, largely due to a lack of information about the duration and type of hormonal therapy, as well as the time of initiation in relation to menopausal transition [ 228 , 229 ]. Nevertheless, it is tempting to speculate that estrogen therapy at early stages of menopause can protect against sudden changes in bioenergetic capacity (e.g., the shift from aerobic glycolysis to ketogenic and FA oxidation) thereby modulating AD risk. Beyond bioenergetics, decreased estrogen levels are associated with less efficient brain cholesterol trafficking and Aβ clearance. Notably, CYP46A1—a key regulator of brain cholesterol levels—was shown to promote cognitive function and memory retention in females, but not in males [ 79 ]. Sex differences in Aβ clearance likely reflect age‑related declines in sex hormones, compromising neuroprotection and tilting the balance towards Aβ accumulation [ 230 , 231 ]. ApoE stands in the intersection between lipid metabolism and Aβ clearance: Lipidated ApoE distributes lipids via lipoprotein receptors, whereas lipid‑poor ApoE binds Aβ or facilitates its interaction with receptors/transporters (e.g., low‐density lipoprotein receptor‐related protein 1, LRP1) for clearance [ 232 ]. Why women with APOE ε4 carry a higher risk remains debated and is likely multifactorial: Menopausal shifts in brain lipid/energy balance [ 233 ], heightened inflammatory responses in female microglia [ 234 ] and increased microbleed risk in women [ 235 ] may all contribute. Conclusions AD is increasingly recognized as a disorder in which disrupted lipid metabolism and mitochondrial dysfunction intersect with amyloid and tau pathology. We propose that at the molecular and cellular level, these processes act as mutually reinforcing drivers of degeneration: Lipids shape mitochondrial cristae curvature and thereby energy production essential for synaptic activity, whereas excess lipid accumulation imposes mitochondrial stress. Altered lipid metabolism and mitochondrial organelle interactions also influence Aβ processing and clearance. We suggest that lipid imbalance and mitochondrial vulnerability converge to heighten neuroinflammation, with mitochondria acting as pro‐inflammatory organelles that amplify cellular stress responses. This feed‐forward cycle may constitute the tipping point that accelerates tau pathology and synaptic dysfunction, correlating with the initiation of cognitive decline. This matrix highlights that these processes interact in feed‐forward loops, underscoring the multifactorial and systemic nature of AD pathogenesis. Beyond the brain, systemic metabolic diseases—including diabetes, vascular dysfunction and dyslipidaemia—create chronic metabolic stress that perturbs lipid homeostasis and increases mitochondrial vulnerability. The resilience or failure to adapt to such metabolic imbalance may explain why some individuals with Aβ pathology never progress to clinical manifestations. Our unifying hypothesis is that genetic predisposition and metabolic stress sensitize mitochondria to lipid dysregulation, establishing a cascade that links systemic health with brain pathology. Recognizing lipid–mitochondria crosstalk as a central node of AD pathogenesis holds particular promise for developing tailored interventions for individuals at elevated genetic and metabolic risk. Further perspectives and clinical implications Despite this proposed framework, several knowledge gaps remain. Single‐cell lipidomics and metabolomics will be critical to understanding cell‐type‐specific changes in lipid composition and mitochondrial metabolism. Such insights could clarify how metabolic stress reshapes mitochondrial pathways, how these changes intersect with genetic risk factors, and why some individuals show resilience despite Aβ pathology. The evolution of AI‐based tools to integrate lipidomic, metabolomic and imaging datasets offers a promising strategy to build predictive models of mitochondrial vulnerability and neurodegeneration. Mechanistically, outstanding questions include how altered lipid composition in AD disrupts mitochondrial structure and bioenergetic capacity, how LD–mitochondria–ER interactions regulate stress responses, and how these processes shape inflammatory signalling in glial cells. Mitochondria are increasingly recognized as hubs of immunometabolism, where metabolites both drive and respond to inflammatory cues. Elucidating these pathways in microglia and astrocytes, as well as their modulation by sex‐specific factors such as estrogen loss and APOE ε4 , will be essential to understanding how systemic metabolic stress translates into neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration. From a translational perspective, dysregulated lipid metabolism is a robust risk modifier, reflecting both genetic susceptibility (e.g., APOE ε4 and CYP46A1) and lifestyle factors that affect cholesterol and systemic metabolism. These pathways represent clinically accessible entry points for therapy. Current strategies under study include statins, GLP‐1 agonists, metformin, oxysterol modulators and lifestyle interventions such as diet and exercise (Table 1 ). Yet clinical evidence remains scarce, as most trials are ongoing and inconclusive, whereas amyloid‐targeting drugs show lower efficacy in women and APOE ε4 carriers as well as increased risks. Additional opportunities lie in restoring brain cholesterol turnover (e.g., CYP46A1 activators) and in targeting microglial lipid metabolism by modulating LD formation, enhancing lipid clearance or mitigating ApoE4‐associated toxicity. This underscores the urgent need for alternatives that address lipid metabolism and mitochondrial dysfunction in parallel, with the potential to deliver mechanism‐based interventions that tackle metabolic vulnerabilities underlying AD progression.
